1Sechseläuten · Zurich · the Böögg burns
Folk forecast vs. data  ·  67 years

3The snowman is wrong (and Switzerland is hotter)

4For 500 years Zurich has lit a giant cotton-wool snowman on fire to forecast the summer. 5We finally have the receipts: 67 years of burn times against the Swiss Plateau weather they were supposed to predict. 6The folk rule is exactly backwards. 7The climate signal is loud.

Hook

8The snowman gets a microphone

9On 19 April 2023, a 3.4-metre cotton-wool snowman stood on top of a 10-metre wood pyre in Zurich, was lit at 6 p.m. 10on the dot, and proceeded to take 57 minutes to explode. 11Slow burn = bad summer. 12That summer's mean temperature on the Swiss Plateau was 20.07 °C — one of the hottest on record.

13The snowman is called the Böögg. 14The festival is Sechseläuten, traceable to a Zurich council vote in 1525 and combined with the official burn since 1902. 15The folk rule has been the same for centuries: the faster the head explodes, the warmer the summer.

16The 2023 result wasn't a one-off bad day. 17In 2022 the Böögg burned for 38 minutes and the summer was the second-hottest on record. 18In 2025 it burned for 26.5 minutes and the summer cleared the 19 °C "record" line again. 19Out of the eight years that Swiss meteorologists single out as the obvious hits and misses, the snowman is right exactly once.

Evidence

25The folk rule, in one chart

26The folk rule is a clean, falsifiable claim: faster burn should predict hotter summer. 27The dataset offers 65 paired observations from 1923 to 2025. 28The Pearson correlation between burn duration and summer mean temperature is r = 0.194, with p = 0.121 — not statistically significant, and pointing the wrong way. 29Slower burns are associated, very weakly, with hotter summers.

30Switching to rank-based Spearman correlation — which protects against the 4-minute and 60-minute outliers — moves the headline number to ρ = 0.173 (p = 0.168). 31Sunshine correlations collapse to essentially zero (ρ = 0.005). 32Whatever you measure, the snowman has nothing to say about it.

33On the scatter chart, the cloud of points has no shape. 34Short burns appear in cool years and hot years; long burns appear in cool years and hot years. 35The folk forecast collides with the data exactly the way MeteoSwiss said it would: a poor forecaster, dressed up in 500 years of tradition.

Turn

How wrong, exactly

43Split the 65 burns at the median (13 minutes). The 32 fast-burn years average 17.49 °C summers. The 33 slow-burn years average 17.64 °C — a difference of −0.148 °C, in the wrong direction, with t = −0.45 and p = 0.66. 46By the rule's own logic, only 2 of 32 fast burns (6.3 %) were actually followed by a record summer; 8 of 33 slow burns (24.2 %) were.

47Slow burns predict record summers four times more often than fast ones.

48As a binary forecast — "fast burn means record summer" — the folk rule reaches 41.5 % agreement with reality across 65 years. 49The base rate of just always saying not 50a record summer hits 84.6 %. 51The snowman is performing about half as well as silence.

Context

65But the burns themselves keep getting weirder

66Across the recorded burns, the median is 13 minutes and the mean is 17.6, with a long right tail that reaches 60 minutes (1923) and 57 minutes (2023). 67Most of the burns finish quickly — 25 % under 10 minutes — and the long ones are the storytellers.

68Dig into the decades and the duration series doesn't trend monotonically — it spikes. 69The 1950s and 60s averaged 6 to 11 minutes; the 1970s and 80s drifted up to 17–21; the 1990s and 2000s came back down to roughly 10–15; and the 2020s have so far averaged 33.6 minutes per burn. 70That's more than five times the 1950s. 71MeteoSwiss attributes the wild swings to entirely earthly things: pyre construction, firewood humidity, weather on the festival day, and the amount of accelerant the guild uses. 72None of this carries information about June.

Turn

77And the summers really are getting hotter

78Now look at what the snowman cannot see. 79Linear regression of summer mean temperature on year, across the same 67 rows, gives a slope of +0.0415 °C per year, r = 0.675, p = 3.8 × 10⁻¹⁰. The implied 100-year warming is 4.15 °C. The first 30 observed years averaged 16.67 °C; the last 30 averaged 18.53 °C. A 1.86 °C gap, in lived memory.

83Cut the same series by decade. 84The 1920s through the 1970s contain zero summers above the 19 °C "record" line. 86The 1980s, 90s, and 2000s have one each. 87The 2010s have four out of nine. 88The 2020s already have four out of six — 66.7 %. 89What was a generational event in the snowman's first half-century is now the most likely outcome of any given July.

90The ten record summers in the dataset that could 91be paired with a burn time sit across the entire duration spectrum: from 5.7 minutes (2003) to 57 minutes (2023). 92The Böögg has been to all of them. 93It just hasn't seen any.

103What we keep watching

104In 2020 the festival was cancelled by COVID. 105In 2024, for the first time ever in the modern era, the burn itself was called off because the wind was too high — gusts of 60–80 km/h across the canton. 106Both years the snowman was silent and both years still happened: 2024 cleared 19 °C and joined the record column anyway.

107The forecast doesn't work. 108The ritual still does. 109Tens of thousands of people stand on Sechseläutenplatz every spring to watch a giant cotton-wool snowman take whatever time it takes to detonate. 110The dataset is a record of what we wanted the weather to be, sitting on top of what it actually became.
