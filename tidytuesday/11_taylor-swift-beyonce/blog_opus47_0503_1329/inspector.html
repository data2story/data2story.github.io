3Beyoncé and Taylor Swift, as two corpora

4A 391-song lyric file, an eight-album sentiment line, and a sales table — and a pair of headline framings that flip when you do the arithmetic.

5Analysis based on the TidyTuesday lyric, sales, and chart-position release of 29 September 2020.

6In September 2020, the TidyTuesday community was handed two artists' catalogs as four CSV files. 7Beyoncé's lyric file: 22,616 lines, 391 song entries. 8Taylor Swift's: 132 songs, eight studio albums. 9The size gap is real, but it's an accident of how the two corpora were built — Beyoncé's includes every remix, live cut and a Lemonade film script; Taylor's is one canonical version per studio track.

10The two artists are paired here for the same reason they have been paired for a decade: the 2009 VMAs, where Kanye interrupted Taylor to argue that Beyoncé deserved the award, then Beyoncé returned the moment to her on stage during her own win. 11The dataset's cover image is a backstage photo from that night.

12Strip the catalog down to numbers and the popular framing of who writes what about love starts to give way.

14The 3.3x lyric gap is built into the dataset

15Compare the two artists at the song level: Beyoncé averages 57.8 lines per song across 391 entries, Taylor 52.7 lines across her 132 studio recordings. 16On a per-song basis the volume is almost identical.

17But six of the ten longest entries in Beyoncé's lyric file are not pop songs at all. 18The longest is the 336-line script of the Lemonade film. 19Three of the next four are different live and remix versions of "Get Me Bodied" (245, 195, and 195 lines). 20The first canonical solo studio track to crack the top 10 by line count is "That's How You Like It" with Jay-Z, at 122 lines.

21The asymmetry is built into the source: Beyoncé's data came from Genius's per-line table (one row per lyric line, including remixes and live transcripts); Taylor's came from a per-song scrape (one row per song, eight studio albums, no remixes). 22The headline "Beyoncé has 3.3 times as many lyric lines" is a fact about the dataset, not the music.

2548 chants vs 12 hooks

26When you ask each artist's lyric file the same simple question — what is the most-repeated single line within one song? 27— the answers describe two different songwriting modes. 28Beyoncé's top within-song repeat is "That's how you like it, huh", sung 48 times in a single track. 29The next is "Who run the world? 30Girls!" at 38 repetitions in one club remix, then 25, then 23.

31Taylor Swift's top within-song repeat is 12. 32Six of her songs are tied at that number — "Look what you just made me do", "Come back, be here", "I'd never walk Cornelia Street again", "Welcome to New York", "All you had to do was stay", "In a getaway car". 33She caps her hooks because there is always another verse to come. 34Beyoncé builds songs out of a single chant.

Beyoncé says “love” more than Taylor does

38Here is the result that contradicts the popular framing the most directly. 39Beyoncé's lyrics are denser with love-words (love, loved, loving, heart, baby, kiss) than Taylor Swift's. 40After stripping stop-words, 3.50% of Beyoncé's content vocabulary is love-words. 41For Taylor, it is 2.36%. 42Beyoncé's rate is 1.5 times Taylor's.

43Look at the raw counts and the picture sharpens. 44"Love" appears 1,362 times in Beyoncé's catalog, 248 in Taylor's. 45"Baby" — 1,024 vs 153. 46"Kiss" and "heart" follow the same pattern. 47Taylor Swift writes a lot about relationships, but the inside of the relationship — the words for the body, the closeness, the moment — belongs to Beyoncé. 48"Halo" alone is sung 383 times across her dataset.

49This is the lexicon-level signal that Rosie Baillie's tidytext-style analysis was set up to find: words count, in aggregate, even when the intuition cuts the other way.

53Bodies and rooms vs landscapes and daylight

54If you compute a log-likelihood ratio over the two artists' shared vocabulary, the words that uniquely belong to each tell their own story. 55Beyoncé's most-distinctive words are "bodied" (205 vs 0), "halo" (383 vs 1), "slay" (148 vs 0), "diva" (110 vs 0), "ladies" (269 vs 2), "upgrade" (86 vs 0). 56Her vocabulary names the body and the room.

57Taylor's most-distinctive words are "starlight" (0 vs 22), "wonderland" (0 vs 21), "getaway" (0 vs 21), "woods" (1 vs 38), "daylight" (2 vs 40), "darling" (5 vs 30), "york" (3 vs 38). 58Her vocabulary names landscapes and times of day. 59One songwriter is in the room with you. 60The other is describing where you both are.

63Folklore is the only sad Taylor album

64Score every Taylor Swift studio album by its sentiment ratio — positive words minus negative, divided by total — and you get a steady arc. 65The self-titled debut: +0.88. 66Fearless: +0.99. 67Speak Now: +1.02 (the most positive album she has made). 68Red, 1989, reputation, Lover all stay net-positive but trend down: +0.92, +0.77, +0.45, +0.41.

69In 2020, the line crosses zero for the first time. 70Folklore — written and recorded in lockdown — is the only Taylor Swift studio album that scores net-negative: 140 positive words against 150 negative, in 4,856 total. 71Speak Now is the most hopeful album she has made; folklore, ten years and one pandemic later, is the first sad one.

72Beyoncé's full catalog scores +1.63 on the same scale. 73Above every Taylor Swift album except Speak Now, Fearless and Red. 74The "breakup album" framing of Lemonade, viewed at the catalog level, doesn't outweigh the love-and-celebration density of the rest.

81Different markets, different #1s

82On the album charts, the two artists own different territory. 83In the US, Beyoncé hits #1 with every one of her six studio albums (100%); Taylor with seven of her eight (87.5%, the lone miss being her 2006 debut). 84In Australia, the order flips: Taylor goes 75% to Beyoncé's 33%. 85New Zealand: Taylor 87.5%, Beyoncé 17%. 86Canada: Taylor 87.5%, Beyoncé 50%. 87France is no one's country — both at 0%.

88At the album level, Beyoncé's median peak position across ten national charts traces a U-shape: she opens at 1.5 with Dangerously in Love, dips to 4.5 with I Am... 89Sasha Fierce in 2008, then climbs back to a perfect 1.0 with Lemonade. 90Taylor's line is the opposite — it falls steadily from a median peak of 38 on the debut, hits 1.0 with Red in 2012, and stays at 1.0 for every album since.

93Per album, the sales gap nearly disappears

94The popular framing — "Taylor outsells Beyoncé worldwide" — is technically true. 95Taylor's six worldwide-row albums total 40.8 million units; Beyoncé's five total 34.5 million. 96But on a per-release basis, the gap nearly vanishes. 97Taylor averages 6.80 million worldwide units per album. 98Beyoncé averages 6.90 million.

99The single largest-selling album in either catalog is Taylor's Fearless (2008) at 12.0 million worldwide. 100Right behind it: Beyoncé's debut Dangerously in Love (2003) at 11.0 million. 101Taylor's 1989 (10.1M), Beyoncé's B'Day (8.0M), Beyoncé's I Am... 102Sasha Fierce (8.0M), Taylor's Red (6.0M). 103Every blockbuster album in the dataset belongs to one of these two artists.

108What the corpus says back

109The two artists were paired here, in 2020, on the assumption that they were doing comparable things. 110The data says they are doing different things, in different vocabularies, with different shapes of repetition, in different markets. 111Beyoncé says love more often per word; Taylor stays positive across thirteen years and then writes folklore.

112The tidytext approach has a tendency to flatten that difference into a single sentiment number. 113The lexicons it ships with were trained on standard English, and they systematically misread Black Vernacular English ("bad" meaning "good"). 114So the +1.63 on Beyoncé's catalog is, almost certainly, an undercount.

115The honest move is to take the lyrics as the lyrics and let the per-album lines tell their own story. 116Speak Now is the brightest. 117Folklore is the first one that goes dark. 118And every blockbuster album in either catalog still belongs to one of these two women.
