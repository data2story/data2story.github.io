2What Kills Small-Plane Pilots

Fifteen years of NTSB findings, 2010–2024. 4A data piece about the gap between accidents that are common 5and accidents that are lethal.

6The 95% number

In the National Transportation Safety Board’s published findings for 17,525 small-airplane accidents between 2010 and 2024, one cause-language pattern stands out from everything else. 8When NTSB investigators wrote that a pilot lost spatial orientation — usually inside a cloud or at night without visible horizon — the result was a fatality 95.0% of the time. That phrase shows up in only 298 of the agency’s probable-cause reports. It carries 558 of the period’s roughly 3,200 deaths.

11Engine failure, by contrast, is the thing pilots are explicitly trained to fear. It shows up in 3,876 NTSB reports — almost a quarter of the dataset. 13And it is fatal 12.7% of the time. 14The crowd of small-aircraft accidents the public sees on local news as a “plane went down” is mostly forced landings into fields and trees, not these. 15The kind of accident that kills almost everyone aboard is rare, specific, and largely invisible to the general reader.

16The headline shape: 17.9% of the aircraft involved in NTSB-investigated Part-91 accidents over 15 years had at least one fatality. 17The other 82% killed no one. 18The story is in the gap between those two numbers — what makes an accident land in the 18% versus the 82%.

What you’re looking at

The dataset is the NTSB’s complete public Aviation Accident Database, filtered to civil flights conducted under 14 CFR Part 91 — the regulatory regime for non-commercial small-aircraft flying, the private side of American aviation — between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2024. 27Each row is the formal output of a federal accident investigation; the narratives field is the agency’s actual probable-cause language, not a journalist’s summary. 28Coverage on the structural fields a reader needs is essentially complete, and 98.5% of events have a probable-cause text attached.

The fleet is overwhelmingly fixed-wing airplanes — 88% — with helicopters next at 7% and a long tail of gliders, balloons, weight-shift trikes, gyrocopters, ultralights and powered-parachutes filling out the rest. When you hear someone say “small-plane crash,” nine times out of ten they mean a single-engine piston airplane, often a Cessna or a Piper of a certain age.

What this is not 31is commercial aviation. 32American airlines have flown for years on end without a passenger fatality on U.S. soil. 33Part 91 has not. 34Most of the small-plane crashes that show up in local news happen here — under a regulatory regime that more closely resembles driving a car than catching a flight on Delta, with a single pilot, no second-in-command, weather decisions made by the person flying the airplane, and a personal-flying mission in nearly three out of four cases.

Fifteen years

37Across the fifteen-year window, the total number of investigated Part-91 accidents fell from 1,344 in 2010 to 1,038 in 2024 — a 22.8% drop. 38The share that were fatal stayed roughly flat at 15–21%, with 2024’s 15.2% the lowest in the series. 39The decline is real but it is mostly fewer accidents happening, not crashes that would once have been fatal becoming survivable. The FAA’s industry benchmark moved in the same direction — fatal accidents per 100,000 flight hours fell from 1.27 in 2001 to roughly 0.65 in 2023 — a slow grind of compounding safety improvement spread across pilots, airframes, training, and avionics.

41The seasonality is sneaky. 42Total accidents peak in July (2,113 in fifteen Julys) and bottom out in December (953) — pilots simply fly more in the summer. But the share 43of accidents that kill someone runs the opposite direction: December (22.4%), November (21.3%) and January (19.4%) are the worst three months for fatal share, exactly when daylight is short and instrument-condition weather is common. 44The chart that makes this work is one with months on the x-axis and two series: counts as bars, fatal share as a line. They cross.

48Common is not lethal

49If you put every probable-cause language pattern on a chart where the x-axis is “how often does this appear in NTSB findings?” and the y-axis is “when it appears, how often does someone die?”, four clusters emerge. In the upper-left — rare and almost always fatal — sit spatial disorientation (95% fatal share), structural failure or in-flight breakup (86%), controlled flight into terrain (85%), stall-spin combinations (82%), and alcohol or drug impairment (82%). In the upper-right — common and very often fatal — sit pure loss of control in-flight (1,646 events / 51% fatal) and aerodynamic stall (1,420 / 50%). In the lower-right — common but mostly survivable — sit engine failure (3,876 / 13%), landing-phase accidents (6,940 / 4%), and runway excursions (990 / less than 1%). 53The lower-left is empty, because the NTSB does not investigate trivial accidents.

54What this shape tells you is that the things that kill GA pilots are not the things that make a runway useless. 55The plane that veers off the centerline at touchdown will be in the database tomorrow morning; the people in it will not. 56The plane that the pilot loses spatial orientation in over a cloud deck at night will be in the database, and so will the obituaries. 57Phase-of-flight reinforces this: landing mentions are 40% of cause-text coverage but 4% fatal, takeoff mentions 15% / 18% fatal, and maneuvering is 5% of mentions but 55% fatal. 58Where in a flight the airplane stops behaving as the pilot expected matters as much as why.

The “loss of control in-flight” cluster — LOC-I in the aviation-safety acronym — is what the NTSB and the General Aviation Joint Steering Committee have spent two decades chasing on their Most Wanted Lists. 60The 1,646 events of straight LOC-I, plus the 1,420 aerodynamic stalls, plus the 235 stall-spins, plus the 298 spatial-disorientation cases — these patterns overlap heavily but, taken together, account for over 1,800 of the 3,165 fatal aircraft-row entries in the dataset. 61More than half of all deaths in fifteen years are concentrated in fewer than ten language patterns.

Into the clouds

Only 4.1% of investigated accidents — 712 events — happen in instrument meteorological conditions (clouds, fog, severely reduced visibility). 68But of those 712, 462 are fatal. 69The fatal share in IMC is 64.9%, against 15.4% in VMC. The ratio inside the dataset is 4.2×; the per-flight-hour ratio in the aviation-safety literature is closer to 14×, because most GA pilots fly almost no IMC. 71The accidents that happen in clouds are very nearly five times more likely to kill someone than the ones that happen in clear air.

72The most dangerous specific combination is the one the NTSB has been warning about for decades: a pilot flying under visual flight rules ends up in instrument conditions. Structurally — weather logged as IMC, flight plan filed as anything other than IFR — there are 336 such events in the dataset. 7472.9% are fatal. 75With no flight plan filed at all (NONE), 275 events and a 74.5% fatal share. 76With a filed VFR plan, 27 events and 66.7% fatal. 77The 1954 University of Illinois study that found non-instrument-rated pilots lose control in 178 seconds of entering cloud is sixty years old; the data says it is still describing what happens.

78What the same chart shows, almost as an afterthought, is that the comparable IMC-with-IFR-plan case — instrument-rated pilot flying on an IFR plan, still in IMC — is fatal 58% of the time. 79Instrument flying does not make IMC safe in absolute terms. 80It makes it survivable enough that airline-scale operations work. 81The gap between IFR-in-IMC and VFR-in-IMC is the difference between a system designed for clouds and a system improvised in clouds.

After dark

84Ninety percent of investigated accidents happen in daylight, with a 16.2% fatal share. 85The night sub-bucket is 6.4% of accidents (1,119 events combined) but its fatal share is 37.4% — 2.3× the daylight rate. 86Inside night, the difference between an ordinary lit-up night and a dark night without moon or ground lights is even sharper: the “dark night” subcategory (NDRK, 313 events) is 55.6% fatal. A “bright” moonlit night is 9.5% — actually safer than the daytime baseline.

88Dawn and dusk twilight are not worse than full daylight in fatal share (17.7% and 18.3%). 89The pattern that emerges is not about low ambient light per se but about the absence of visible reference outside the cockpit — exactly the conditions in which spatial disorientation occurs. 90Looking back at the centerpiece chart: spatial disorientation appears in 95% of cases that involve it as a fatal, and the conditions that produce it are the same conditions you see in this chart’s worst bar.

The Alaska paradox

93California (1,575 events), Texas (1,513), and Florida (1,385) lead the absolute counts, in roughly the order their GA fleets size them. Alaska is fourth with 1,016 events — 5.8% of the U.S. 95dataset, despite having less than 1% of the country’s population. 96That is roughly seven to eight times the lower-48 per-capita rate, consistent with longstanding NTSB and academic findings that Alaska’s accident rate per flight hour is approximately double the national rate.

97But here the data does something the Detective-stage research did not predict. Alaska’s per-accident fatal share is only 9.6% — about half the lower-48 rate of 18.2%. 99Said differently: Alaska has roughly twice the rate of accidents and roughly half the rate of fatal outcomes per accident. 100The likely explanation, given the conditions described in the bush-flying literature, is what bush-flying communities have always understood: a lot of Alaskan GA flying is short-distance, low-altitude, low-speed work onto unimproved strips, ridges, sandbars, and water. 101Those crashes ruin airframes; they often spare the people inside. 102The state has more accidents and more survivable ones, both for the same reason.

108Old planes, old patterns

109Three manufacturers account for half of every accident: Cessna 27.3%, Piper 16.8%, Beech 6.3%. 110The Cessna 172 alone, in its various dash-letter variants, accounts for over 1,800 events — a function of the airplane being the most-built civilian aircraft in history (over 44,000 produced since 1956). 111Fatal share within accidents, though, is highest among the high-performance singles: Beech, Cirrus, and Mooney each cluster around 26–28% fatal — well above Cessna’s 13.8% — consistent with their faster cruise speeds and tighter handling margins.

112One contrast worth being careful about is the homebuilt premium. 113Outside research puts the experimental / amateur-built fatal accident rate per flight hour at roughly four times the certificated rate. The fatal share inside this dataset’s accidents is 22.2% for homebuilts and 17.3% for factory aircraft — a 1.28× ratio. These are different statistics: the 4× per-hour ratio includes both higher accident frequency and higher severity-per-accident; the 1.28× ratio is just the severity-per-accident leg of it. 117Both are real, both are higher than baseline, but the headline number to use depends on the question.

What it adds up to

139Three numbers do most of the work. 140Maneuvering: 5% of cause-text mentions, 55% fatal. 141Night-dark: 1.8% of accidents, 56% fatal. 142IMC with no IFR plan: 1.6% of accidents, 75% fatal. 143None of them is the kind of thing that appears in pilot conversations about engine reliability or airframe maintenance. They are about decisions — to keep flying into deteriorating visibility, to take off into a night with no moon, to follow the road at low altitude to see something on the ground. 145The crashes that kill people are largely the crashes the pilot chose to set up.

149The single deadliest event in the dataset is the July 2016 hot-air-balloon crash near Lockhart, Texas — sixteen people, on a sightseeing flight that took off in fog. The NTSB’s published probable cause, quoted above, is a single sentence that contains most of what this analysis has found.

151The next-most-deadly events follow similar patterns at smaller scales: an “aggressive takeoff maneuver, which resulted in an accelerated stall and subsequent loss of control at an altitude that was too low for recovery.” A failure “to maintain airplane control following a reduction of thrust in the left engine during takeoff.” A pilot’s “inadequate decision” to continue. These are the federal investigators’ words, not interpretations stacked on top. 153They are what the database actually says.

154Common is not lethal and lethal is not common. 155Engine failures are common and survivable; spatial disorientation is rare and almost certain to kill. 156The story for the next decade of GA safety improvement — the one the NTSB has been writing in its Most Wanted Lists since at least 2014 — is whether anything can be done about the rare-and-lethal upper-left corner of the chart. 157The lower-right corner has already, slowly, given way to fewer accidents and lower per-hour rates. 158The upper-left is still there.
