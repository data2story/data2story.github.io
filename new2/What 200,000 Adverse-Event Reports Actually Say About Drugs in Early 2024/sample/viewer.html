4The top complaint isn't medical

5Among 597,802 adverse-reaction rows in FAERS for the first seven weeks of 2024, the single most-reported MedDRA Preferred Term is not nausea, not pain, not death. 6It is "Off label use" — 14,801 mentions, 2.48% of all reaction rows. 7The next two terms are "Drug ineffective" (11,335) and "Death" itself logged as a reaction (9,356). 8The first recognisably medical symptom — "Fatigue" — appears in fourth place.

9Fully 1.8% of every single report in the dataset is one person filing one complaint: that the drug did not work. 10No symptom, no event, no outcome — just "Drug ineffective" and nothing else. 11That is 3,554 reports out of 200,000, and another 7,683 reports that mention "Drug ineffective" alongside something else.

12FAERS is fed by anyone who notices a problem — patients writing in to MedWatch, hospital safety officers, drug manufacturers fulfilling mandatory reporting, clinical-trial sites. 13Many of those reports are not medical at all. 14They describe how the drug was used, whether it worked, whether the right dose came out of the pen. 15The database's loudest signal is paperwork, not pharmacology.

27What FAERS is, and what its biggest numbers actually mean

28FAERS is the FDA's central post-market surveillance database. 29Anyone with a story about a drug — a patient who developed a rash, a clinician who saw an unexpected outcome, a manufacturer required by law to forward a complaint — can submit a report. 30Every report names a patient (age, sex, country), one or more drugs (each marked as Suspect, Concomitant, or Interacting), and one or more reactions described in MedDRA, the standardized vocabulary used by drug regulators worldwide. 31The full 2024 universe is roughly 1.32 million reports.

32The slice in front of us is 200,000 reports drawn chronologically through the OpenFDA API — Jan 1 through Feb 23 only, the first 54 days of 2024. 33About 15% of the year's volume sits in this snapshot. 34The patient fields are sparser than you would hope: age is missing 40% of the time, sex 16%, country of occurrence 11%. 35The seriousness flag is almost always present.

36Half the reports — 52.5% — are tagged "serious", an extraordinarily high rate by epidemiological standards. 37Nine percent record a death. 38A fifth involve a hospitalization. 39None of this means the drugs are killing or hospitalising people at those rates. 40It means the events that get reported to FAERS skew strongly toward the events worth reporting: hospital workups, life-threatening reactions, adverse outcomes severe enough that someone wrote them down.

41FAERS has no denominator. 42The database does not know how many patients took each drug. 43A report count cannot be divided by a prescription count, and the FDA itself states that the system is for "generating hypotheses about potential safety signals," not for measuring rates.

53Dupixent and Mounjaro are tied for #1

54Across all 776,793 drug appearances in the dataset, Dupixent — dupilumab, an injectable biologic for atopic dermatitis and asthma — is the most-reported product, with 15,027 mentions and 1.93% of every drug row. 55Mounjaro (tirzepatide, the type-2-diabetes blockbuster from Eli Lilly) is the closest possible second at 14,908 mentions and 1.92%. 56The two are separated by 119 rows out of three-quarters of a million.

57When the analysis is narrowed to drugs marked as the suspect product — the drug the reporter thinks caused the event — Dupixent still edges out Mounjaro, 14,916 to 14,742. 58Together they make up just under 6% of all suspect-drug rows in the dataset. 59The remaining top of the list is recognisable to anyone who works in pharmacovigilance: Xolair, Repatha, Cabometyx, methotrexate, Rituximab, Humira — the immunology and oncology biologics that have anchored FAERS for years.

60Behind that ranking is a sharper diagnostic. 61Some drugs are almost always the suspect when they appear: Dupixent 99.3% suspect, Mounjaro 98.9%, Xolair 99.1%, Cabometyx 99.8%. 62Others are almost never the suspect — aspirin is the suspect in only 15.8% of its appearances, atorvastatin in 21.7%, acetaminophen in 49.0%. 63Those drugs show up because the patient was taking them at the time, not because they are blamed for anything. 64A drug's headline count is half story, half background noise; the role mix tells you which.

67The GLP-1 surge is real, but the spotlight is on the wrong drug

68The press story of 2024 has been Ozempic — semaglutide, the GLP-1 receptor agonist that became a household word for weight loss. 69In the FAERS sample, however, Ozempic ranks 21st by total mentions. 70The dominant GLP-1 drug is Mounjaro at 14,908 rows, with 3,077 more for its sibling Zepbound (tirzepatide approved for obesity in November 2023). 71Pooled across brands, tirzepatide produces 18,035 drug rows; semaglutide (Ozempic + Wegovy + Rybelsus) produces 5,425. 72The newer drug outruns the famous one by a factor of 3.3.

73The eight major GLP-1 brand names produce a head-to-head table that looks nothing like a single drug class. 74Mounjaro: 3,159 suspect-reports, 11% tagged serious, 0.7% involving a death. 75Ozempic: 2,493 suspect-reports, 32% serious, 0.6% death. 76Zepbound: 718 suspect-reports, 2.5% serious, zero deaths. 77Wegovy and Saxenda — both indicated for weight loss — show the strongest female skew (82% and 88% female of sex-known reports). 78The diabetes-indicated brands skew older (median age 63 for Ozempic, Rybelsus, Trulicity); the obesity-indicated brands skew younger (47 for Wegovy, 49 for Zepbound, 45.5 for Saxenda).

79Two drugs that share an active ingredient — Mounjaro and Zepbound, both tirzepatide — generate the gentlest seriousness profiles in the dataset. 80The same active ingredient, marketed for diabetes by physicians (Mounjaro) versus marketed for weight loss directly to consumers (Zepbound), produces reports that look mild by FAERS standards. 81It is almost certainly a reporter-community effect: weight-loss patients filing reports through manufacturer pharmacovigilance hotlines, mostly about misfiring pens, not hospitalisations.

86Mounjaro's complaints are about the pen; Ozempic's are about the body

87The top reactions for Mounjaro and Ozempic look almost like reports from different drugs. 88Mounjaro's top complaint is "Incorrect dose administered" — 21.7% of all Mounjaro reports — followed by "Injection site pain" (14.5%), "Off label use" (12.5%), and only then "Nausea" (9.9%). 89Zepbound's top complaints are even more device-focused: "Incorrect dose administered" 21.4%, "Accidental underdose" 16.4%, "Injection site pain" 13.6%. 90Ozempic looks like a different drug. 91Its top reactions are "Nausea" (16.2%), "Vomiting" (9.3%), "Diarrhoea" (9.2%), "Constipation" (7.6%), "Decreased appetite" (6.8%). 92The body is the complaint, not the syringe.

93Stepping back across all 10,056 GLP-1 reports, the reactions most over-represented in the class versus the dataset average tell the underlying story. 94"Impaired gastric emptying" appears 14.8 times more often in GLP-1 reports than would be expected from base rates. 95"Pancreatitis" — a long-recognised GLP-1 risk — appears 8.4 times more often. 96"Glycosylated haemoglobin increased" appears 9.7 times more often, meaning some patients are complaining the drug failed to bring their HbA1c down. 97And then, in the same ranked list: "Weight loss poor" (18.5x lift) and "Increased appetite" (11.1x). 98The paradox of the class shows up directly — both the patients whose appetite vanishes and the patients whose appetite returns are writing in.

103The actual death-rate hotspots are nowhere near the headlines

104Filtering to drugs with at least 200 suspect-reports and ranking by share of reports involving a death produces a list that has almost nothing to do with weight-loss drugs. 105The top entries: methamphetamine (93.5% of its suspect-reports involve a death), alcohol (89.4%), fentanyl (71.9%), diphenhydramine (70.9%), trazodone (69.1%), cocaine (66.1%), oxycodone (57.9%). 106These are not signals that prescribed drugs are killing patients. 107They are reports filed after toxicology found the substance present at the time of death — overdose cases, polypharmacy mortality, drug-related fatalities being routed into FAERS because a drug was named in the chart. 108Notably absent from the top thirty: any GLP-1 drug.

109Among the 39,213 reactions explicitly tagged with a Fatal outcome, the most common identifiable cause of death is "Completed suicide" (1,602 rows, 4.1% of fatal reactions). 110It is followed by "Toxicity to various agents" (2.7%), "Drug abuse" (2.2%), cardio-respiratory arrest, pneumonia, cardiac arrest, sepsis. 111FAERS death numbers are real, but they are death-from-anything-while-on-a-drug numbers — they are not a leaderboard of drugs that kill.

114Who files, when, and from where

115FAERS reports skew strongly female. 116Of reports with a known patient sex, 59.5% are about women and 40.5% are about men — a 1.47:1 ratio. 117The skew shows up in every drug analysed individually: Wegovy 82% female, Dupixent's autoimmune-leaning population female-dominated, even Mounjaro 74% female despite the underlying diabetic population being roughly balanced.

121Where the events happen is, overwhelmingly, the United States. 12260.3% of reports describe an event that occurred in the US; the next closest is Canada at 5.1%, then the UK at 3.5%, Japan at 3.3%, France at 3.3%. 123The 99.9% match between event country and reporter country shows that FAERS is fed locally — clinicians and patients in each country file about events in their own country, and manufacturers forward those reports through their pharmacovigilance teams. 124The US dominance is not because Americans get more adverse events; it is because Americans are far more likely to file a report when they have one.

125Eighty-one percent of reports come in through spontaneous channels — the canonical patient/clinician/manufacturer-forwarded route. 126Sixteen percent come from clinical studies, three percent from "Other" channels (literature, surveys). 127The Study channel is small but disproportionately serious: oncology-trial safety reports come through it.

128The data has a heartbeat. 129Across the 54-day window, weekdays receive an average of 35,000–40,000 reports while weekends crater to 5,500 (Saturday) and 8,200 (Sunday). 130On every Saturday and Sunday the count drops 80–90% from the surrounding Friday. 131Adverse events do not stop happening on weekends. 132The reporting infrastructure does. 133FAERS is not a real-time monitor; it is a five-day-a-week intake queue.

141How to read the next FAERS headline

142The two findings that survive every caveat are these. 143First: the top "adverse reaction" in the database is not a medical event, it is "Off label use" — and the second is "Drug ineffective". 144Second: the GLP-1 brand at the centre of the next round of headlines is much more likely to be Mounjaro than Ozempic, because Mounjaro is generating three times more reports already, and most of those reports are about pen handling, not pharmacology.

145Every number in this blog is a count of reports, not a count of events. 146FAERS is a megaphone, not a measurement. 147A drug that has been in the news collects more reports for the same underlying rate. 148A drug whose patients are old, female, and U.S.-based collects more reports per prescription than one whose patients are not. 149A drug whose pen is hard to use collects more "Incorrect dose administered" reports than one whose pen is not.

150The FDA itself reads FAERS this way. 151Each quarter the agency publishes a list of "Potential Signals of Serious Risks/New Safety Information" identified from these reports — sometimes only dozens of cases — that warrant closer investigation. 152The signals trigger label changes, dose warnings, drug-safety communications. 153Nothing in FAERS is causation, but everything in FAERS is a question worth asking.

154Read the next news cycle about an "adverse event database surge" with the structure in mind. 155A spike in reports means someone is filing them — a regulator put out a warning, a class-action lawyer ran an ad, a TV doctor mentioned the drug. 156The events behind the reports may or may not have changed. 157The reporting always has.
