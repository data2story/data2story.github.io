9The Meteoritical Society catalogues 45,716 named meteorites. 10Only 1,107 of them — 2.42% — were ever observed during the fall itself. 11The rest are Finds: rocks recognised on the ground at some unknown later date, sometimes thousands of years after they landed.

12The witnessing rate is roughly six observed falls per year worldwide, and it has been roughly six per year for as long as anyone has been counting. 13The Earth gets hit by an estimated 1,800 land-falls every year. 14So the chance that any given fall enters the catalogue as a witnessed event is well under one percent.

15Everything else this blog covers is a consequence of that gap.

21The Find explosion of 1969

22The cleanest way to see the catalogue's shape is to split it at 1970. 23Before 1970, the dataset records 1,431 Finds. 24After 1970, it records 42,886 — a thirty-fold jump that is the single biggest pattern in the data.

25Decade-by-decade counts make the timing impossible to miss. 26Through the 1960s, Finds tick along at a few hundred per decade. 27In the 1970s the number jumps to 4,909. 28In the 1990s it crosses ten thousand. 29In the 2000s alone, more meteorites enter the catalogue (17,698) than in the entire previous century combined.

30Nothing changed in the sky in 1969. 31What changed is that the first systematic Antarctic search began. 32Japanese and then American teams started driving snowmobiles across blue-ice fields where katabatic winds had spent millennia scouring meteorites back to the surface. 33ANSMET alone has now recovered more than 23,000 specimens — more than half of every meteorite humans have ever named.

38A map of where humans looked

39Among the 38,400 catalogue rows with coordinates, 22,099 — 59.2% — are below latitude -60. 40All 22,099 are Finds. 41The Antarctic ice contributes more than half of every geocoded meteorite humans have ever recovered, and zero observed falls.

42Strip Antarctica away and the remaining geography is itself almost all desert. 43The Arabian peninsula and Oman supply 3,154 rocks; the Sahara and Sahel 2,414; the US southwest 1,063; the Australian outback 540; the Atacama 415. 44The single hottest five-degree cell outside Antarctica is the corner of West Africa where longitude zero crosses the Sahara, with more than six thousand entries.

45The pattern is not that meteorites prefer cold or dry land. 46It is that on cold or dry land they stand out, and on cold or dry land humans now go looking.

54Ten orders of magnitude, log-normal

55Meteorite masses span more than ten orders of magnitude — from sub-gram chips to the sixty-tonne Hoba. 56The median rock weighs 32.7 grams (about a tomato). 57The arithmetic mean is 13.3 kilograms (a cinder block), four hundred times larger than the median. 58That ratio is the canonical signature of a heavy-tailed distribution: a small number of giants and an army of pebbles.

59The asymmetry shows up sharply across the Fell-vs-Found split. 60Median Fell mass is 2,800 grams; median Found mass is 30.5 grams. 61A typical witnessed meteorite is ninety-two times heavier than a typical recovered one. 62That makes physical sense: the only meteorites loud and bright enough to be seen mid-fall are the big ones. 63Antarctic and desert searches, by contrast, pick up gram-scale chips an eyewitness would never even notice.

64The top 20 are a wall of iron

65Sort the catalogue by mass and the top twenty rows are a wall of iron. 66Hoba (60 tonnes, Namibia), Cape York (58 tonnes, Greenland), Campo del Cielo (50 tonnes, Argentina), Canyon Diablo (30 tonnes, Arizona, the body that punched out Meteor Crater), Armanty (28 tonnes, Xinjiang). 67Every one is an iron meteorite. 68The first stony rock to appear — the Jilin H5 chondrite that fell on China in 1976 — does not show up until rank nineteen.

69Widen the window to the top hundred and the bias holds: seventy-two of the heaviest hundred meteorites in the catalogue are irons, ten are stony-irons, only fifteen are ordinary chondrites. 70Yet irons are barely 2.3% of the catalogue by count. 71The heaviest meteorites on Earth are not a sample of what falls; they are a sample of what survives. 72Iron blocks shrug off atmospheric ablation and weather slowly enough that a 60-tonne lump can sit in a Namibian field for eighty thousand years and still be there to plough into.

73Some of these rocks were the centre of their own stories long before the Meteoritical Society named them. 74The Inughuit of Greenland cold-forged iron tools from the Cape York fragments for centuries before Robert Peary sledged the largest three off the ice in the 1890s and sold the biggest one to the American Museum of Natural History for forty thousand dollars. 75Hoba was discovered in 1920 by a Namibian farmer ploughing a field; it was declared a national monument before anyone tried to move it.

82Counts say chondrite, mass says iron

83By row count, the catalogue is dominated by one composition family. 84Ordinary chondrites — H, L, and LL groups with petrologic types 3 through 6 — make up 86.96% of all entries (39,756 of 45,716). 85Carbonaceous chondrites are a distant second at 3.50%, followed by the HED achondrites at 2.59% and iron meteorites at 2.34%. 86The dataset's own breakdown matches the literature's "87% ordinary chondrite" benchmark almost to the decimal.

87Now flip the lens to mass per row. 88Iron meteorites carry a median mass of 10,000 grams — ten kilograms. 89Ordinary chondrites' median is 28.5 grams. 90The typical iron is three hundred and fifty times heavier than the typical chondrite. 91So irons are 1 in 43 rows by count, but they dominate the heavy tail. 92The same flip explains why an analysis based on the top of the chart and an analysis based on row counts produce two completely different pictures of "what a meteorite is."

93Witness rates by family expose a quieter selection effect. 94Iron meteorites and HED achondrites are observed falling at 4.6% and 5.1% respectively — roughly double the catalogue's overall 2.4% rate. 95Ordinary chondrites sit at the mean (2.1%). 96Lunar achondrites are 0.0%: none of the 165 lunar meteorites in the catalogue has ever been seen falling. 97Every one of them was recovered from Antarctic ice or hot-desert pavement, identified later in a lab.

98The catalogue is a record of where we looked

99Decade-by-decade, the Antarctic share of the catalogue has its own history. 100In the 1970s and 1980s, blue-ice teams supplied 89-94% of every dated geocoded rock. 101In the 1990s, hot-desert programs in the Sahara and Arabia caught up; by the 2000s, Antarctica's share had dropped to 39% even as the absolute count kept rising. 102The frozen 2010s tail of the dataset shows the deserts now leading.

103Lay all three numbers next to each other — 2.4% witnessed, 59% Antarctic, 97% post-1969 — and the meteorite catalogue stops looking like an inventory of what hits Earth and starts looking like an inventory of where and when humans looked for what hit Earth a long time ago. 104Witnessed falls are the only rows that approximate a random sample of what is really up there, and there are barely a thousand of them spread across twelve hundred years.

105Whatever the next forty thousand entries look like, the controlling variable will be the same: which search programs run, where, and for how long.
