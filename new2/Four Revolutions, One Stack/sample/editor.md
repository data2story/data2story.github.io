## Story Spine

**Core claim**: Industrial revolutions don't replace fuels — they stack them. Each of the four revolutions added a new primary-energy source on top of the previous ones, and now the fourth is teaching us that "clean energy" can coexist with the largest coal year in human history.

**Tension**: The standard story of energy transitions implies movement from dirty to clean. The data shows movement from dirty to dirtier-plus-some-clean. The world burns more coal in 2024 than at any earlier point, more oil than during the post-war boom, and is building gigawatt training campuses for AI workloads the grid was never designed to serve.

**Payoff**: Stop thinking of the 4th industrial revolution as the clean one. It's the one whose signature fuel is electricity-on-demand, doubling its appetite faster than any prior era.

## Sections

### edt_01: Coal just had its biggest year ever

**Evidence**: ana_03, ana_04, ana_01 | **Context**: det_05

[ana_03] In 2024 the world burned more coal than in any prior year on record — 45,851 TWh of primary energy, up from a then-record 42,016 TWh in 2010. That is five times the volume burned at the supposed apex of the coal era, 1910, when coal accounted for 55% of all human energy use. Coal's share of the global mix has fallen since 1910. Its absolute volume has not.

[ana_04, det_05] The same is true for the rest of the fossil family. Total fossil share peaked in 1973, the year of the OPEC oil shock, at 81.6% of all primary energy. In 2024 it sits at 76.4% — a drop of just over five percentage points across half a century. In the same window, absolute fossil consumption nearly tripled, from 53,000 TWh to 142,000 TWh per year.

[editorial] The mental model where each industrial revolution "switched" the world to a cleaner fuel is wrong. The dataset behind this piece — eight files curated from Our World in Data, Epoch AI, the Bank of England, and the Maddison Project — tells a different story: every revolution added a fuel without retiring the previous one.

### edt_02: The first revolution had a 300-year run-up

**Evidence**: ana_16 | **Context**: det_01, det_10

[ana_16, det_10] English coal output grew 48-fold between 1508 and 1800 — most of it before James Watt's improved steam engine reached commercial scale in 1776. The Bank of England's "Millennium of Macroeconomic Data" series, which extends back to the 13th century, shows the index rising from 8.8 (1508) to 100 (1700) to 421 (1800). The 16th- and 17th-century jumps came from domestic heating, brewing, salt-boiling, and iron-smelting demand that justified building the Newcastle coastal coal trade out of nothing.

[ana_16, det_01] Watt's engine then layered on another 4.2x in the 18th century and a further 4.5x in the first half of the 19th. The point is not that the engine didn't matter — it mattered enormously — but that it accelerated a fuel transition that had already been running for two centuries. The first industrial revolution had a long, slow ramp behind it.

### edt_03: Each revolution had its own new fuel

**Evidence**: ana_02, ana_01 | **Context**: det_01, det_02, det_03, det_04

[ana_02, det_01] The OWID global-energy file dates each fuel's first non-zero year. Coal had already been in the record from 1800; oil enters in 1870, just as the second industrial revolution begins. Natural gas and hydropower both enter in 1890 — by the time Edison's grid was wired up, three new primary-energy sources were already in the global mix.

[ana_02, det_03] The third revolution's fuels show up clustered at 1965: nuclear (71 TWh that year, eleven years after Obninsk's grid connection), along with modern biofuels and other renewables. Wind crosses the threshold in 1978, solar in 1983 — both starting at essentially zero. The fourth revolution's fuels are wind, solar, and the AI-compute electricity demand they are racing to feed.

### edt_04: How fossil never really lost share

**Evidence**: ana_04, ana_03 | **Context**: det_05

[ana_04, det_05] Vaclav Smil has been arguing for forty years that energy transitions take decades — that new sources reach a few percent of the market within a generation, but only displace incumbents over the next two or three. The post-1973 fossil-share trajectory is the cleanest expression of his thesis. Down from 81.6% to 76.4% in 51 years is real, but it is gentle.

[ana_03] Inside the fossil block, coal lost share to oil and gas, but kept growing in absolute terms. Coal at 2024 = 24.6% of primary energy, down from 55% in 1910 — yet 5x the volume burned in 1910. Oil went from 6 TWh in 1870 to 55,292 TWh in 2024. Natural gas, the supposed transition fuel, sits at 41,278 TWh and is still climbing. Each one displaced the rest in share only because the pie kept getting bigger.

### edt_05: The CO2 receipt

**Evidence**: ana_06, ana_07, ana_08 | **Context**: det_05, det_08

[ana_06] Annual world CO2 emissions in 1750 were 9.3 million tonnes, almost entirely from English coal. In 2024 they were 38.6 billion tonnes — a multiplier of 4,148x. Every kink in the global energy mix shows up on the CO2 curve, and only three modest declines are visible across the 274-year record: the 2008-09 financial crisis, a 2014-16 Chinese coal slowdown, and the pandemic dip in 2020. None of the nominal energy transitions — the rise of nuclear, the post-2010 renewables takeoff — register as a break.

[ana_07] The intensity number does, slightly. Tonnes of CO2 per TWh of non-biomass primary energy have fallen from 345,954 in 1850 to 220,221 in 2024 — about a 36% decline over 174 years, or 0.3% per year. Oil and gas are cleaner than coal, and the renewables wedge is effectively zero. But the rate of intensity decline is far slower than what a true energy transition would require: getting the curve to zero needs either a 95%+ intensity cut or an absolute contraction of fossil volume, and the 21st century has so far delivered neither.

[ana_08] Cumulative responsibility for those 1.8 trillion tonnes of country-attributed CO2 is even more concentrated than current flows suggest. The United States alone is 24.1% of the historical total; China is 15.8%, despite producing more annually today than the US ever has. The next tier — Russia, Germany, the UK, Japan — are all early-industrial economies whose 19th- and 20th-century emissions still dominate. India is the world's third-largest current emitter but only seventh cumulatively, because its industrial era started so recently.

### edt_06: Renewables are real, but the world wants more of everything

**Evidence**: ana_05, ana_09 | **Context**: det_04

[ana_05, det_04] Solar and wind together produced 11 TWh of primary energy in 1990 — essentially a rounding error. In 2024 they produced 11,275 TWh, a thousand-fold increase across 34 years. The doubling has not slowed: 990 TWh (2010), 2,755 TWh (2015), 6,057 TWh (2020), 11,275 TWh (2024). Solar caught wind in 2024 and is now the world's fastest-deploying source of new electricity generation.

[ana_09] But renewables are not the only thing growing. Per-capita energy demand is climbing almost everywhere outside the United States and Western Europe. China went from 2,124 kWh per person in 1965 to 34,514 in 2024 — sixteen-fold growth, narrowly crossing the EU average for the first time. India is at 7,813 kWh per person, less than a quarter of the EU level. Africa has barely moved, from 2,228 to 4,055 kWh per person in 60 years.

[ana_09] The benchmark to keep in mind: US per-capita energy in 1965 was 73,802 kWh. China today consumes 47% of that. India 11%. Africa 5%. If the rest of the world reaches even half the US-1965 level by 2050, total global energy demand needs to roughly double again — on top of the renewables buildout. The IEA's most optimistic Renewables 2024 forecast has wind and solar PV reaching 30% of electricity generation by 2030. Electricity is roughly a quarter of total energy. The math is tight.

### edt_07: AI compute is the new fuel demand

**Evidence**: ana_11, ana_12, ana_15 | **Context**: det_06, det_11

[ana_12, det_06] Frontier AI training compute doubled every 19.7 months from the start of machine learning in 1950 through 2009. From 2010 onwards, the doubling time collapsed to 5.7 months — a 3.4x acceleration that lines up almost exactly with Epoch AI's published headline that post-2010 compute is doubling every six months. The 17 years since 2010 contain ten orders of magnitude of growth: the maximum-compute model in 2010 used 5.4e16 FLOP. In 2025 it was 5e26 FLOP — Grok 4, from xAI.

[ana_11] The median model has tracked along: from 1e16 FLOP in 2010 to 3.0e24 FLOP in 2024, a 100-million-fold increase in fourteen years. This is the empirical signature of the fourth industrial revolution as a compute revolution, distinct from but parallel to the renewables buildout. It is the most dramatic single-decade growth curve of any energy-consuming workload in human history.

[ana_15, det_11] Where is the compute coming from? Not, mostly, from the chips themselves. Moore's Law — Intel 4004 to today's 58-billion-transistor chips, a 25-million-fold density gain across 50 years — slowed to a 26.5-month doubling time after 2010, a modest deceleration from the prior 23.5 months. The post-2010 compute curve is roughly five times steeper than the per-chip transistor curve, which means the gap is being closed by parallelism and runtime: more chips deployed per training run, and longer runs.

### edt_08: A single training run draws as much power as a town

**Evidence**: ana_13, ana_14 | **Context**: det_07

[ana_14] More chips and longer runs convert directly to more electricity. Maximum recorded training power draw for an AI model grew from 2.1 kW in 2015 (small academic experiments) to 109.9 MW in 2025. That is roughly a tenfold increase every four years, or doubling annually. 110 MW sustained for weeks or months is in the same league as a steel mill or a mid-sized data center, but concentrated on a single workload.

[ana_13] The 110 MW number belongs to a specific model: Grok 3 from xAI, published 17 February 2025, at 3.5e26 FLOP. The other 100+ MW-scale runs in the database — Llama 4 Behemoth (44 MW), Gemini 1.0 Ultra (38 MW), Llama 3.1-405B (23 MW), GPT-4 (20 MW) — sit just below it. All are from the last three years. The very top of the compute table — Grok 4, GPT-4.5, Grok 3, GPT-5 — collectively burned roughly 10,000 times the training compute of the original GPT-4.

[ana_13, det_07] The IEA's 2025 Energy and AI report projects global data-centre electricity demand growing from ~415 TWh in 2024 to ~945 TWh by 2030 — from 1.5% of world electricity to just under 3%, in six years. Hyperscale AI campuses now routinely contract for 100 MW+ interconnections, with some planned at gigawatt scale. Most regional grids cannot supply this without 5-10 years of transmission upgrades that have not yet been built.

### edt_09: Where the four revolutions leave us

**Evidence**: (none new) | **Context**: det_05

[editorial] Each industrial revolution found a new fuel, scaled it, and left the previous ones in place. Coal did not retire when oil arrived. Oil did not retire when gas arrived. None of them retired when nuclear, solar, or wind arrived. The fourth industrial revolution is no different. Its signature fuel is electricity-on-demand for AI training compute, and the grid is being asked to build out for that workload at the same time it is being asked to absorb a renewables wedge that is already growing as fast as anything in industrial history.

[editorial, det_05] Smil's old observation holds. The wood-to-coal-to-oil-to-gas transition took more than a century. Solar and wind have done roughly a thousand-fold expansion in 34 years and now serve 6% of primary energy. AI training compute is doubling every 5.7 months. These three curves are not parallel — they are running on entirely different clocks. The interesting question for the next ten years is whether the build-out clock can outrun the demand clock.

[editorial] What this dataset cannot answer is whether the answer is yes. It can only show what the stack looks like so far: four revolutions, six primary fuels still in use, 76% fossil, 4,148x the CO2 of 1750, and a new training run starting somewhere right now at 100-plus megawatts.
