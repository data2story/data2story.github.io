3Four Revolutions, One Stack

Industrial revolutions don't replace fuels — they stack them. 5Each revolution added a new primary-energy source on top of the previous ones, leaving us in 2026 burning more coal than ever, while the fourth revolution adds AI training compute as a new 100-megawatt load.

A long-read on eight datasets · 224 years · 1,018 AI models

7Coal just had its biggest year ever

8In 2024 the world burned more coal than in any prior year on record — 945,851 TWh 10of primary energy, up from a then-record 1142,016 TWh 12in 2010. 13That is five times the volume burned at the supposed apex of the coal era, 1910, when coal accounted for 55% of all human energy use. 14Coal's share of the global mix has fallen since 1910. 15Its absolute volume has not.

16The same is true for the rest of the fossil family. 17Total fossil share peaked in 1973, the year of the OPEC oil shock, at 1881.6% 19of all primary energy. 20In 2024 it sits at 2176.4% — a drop of just over five percentage points across half a century. 23In the same window, absolute fossil consumption nearly tripled, from 2453,000 TWh to 25142,000 TWh per year.

26The mental model where each industrial revolution "switched" the world to a cleaner fuel is wrong. The dataset behind this piece — eight files curated from Our World in Data, Epoch AI, the Bank of England, and the Maddison Project — tells a different story: every revolution added a fuel without retiring the previous one.

35The first revolution had a 300-year run-up

36English coal output grew 3748-fold between 1508 and 1800 — most of it before James Watt's improved steam engine reached commercial scale in 1776. 39The Bank of England's "Millennium of Macroeconomic Data" series, which extends back to the 13th century, shows the index rising from 8.8 (1508) to 100 (1700) to 421 (1800). 40The 16th- and 17th-century jumps came from domestic heating, brewing, salt-boiling, and iron-smelting demand that justified building the Newcastle coastal coal trade out of nothing.

41Watt's engine then layered on another 4.2x in the 18th century and a further 4.5x in the first half of the 19th. The point is not that the engine didn't matter — it mattered enormously — but that it accelerated a fuel transition that had already been running for two centuries. 43The first industrial revolution had a long, slow ramp behind it.

46Each revolution had its own new fuel

47The OWID global-energy file dates each fuel's first non-zero year. 48Coal had already been in the record from 1800; oil enters in 1870, just as the second industrial revolution begins. Natural gas and hydropower both enter in 1890 — by the time Edison's grid was wired up, three new primary-energy sources were already in the global mix.

50The third revolution's fuels show up clustered at 1965: nuclear (5171 TWh 52that year, eleven years after Obninsk's grid connection), along with modern biofuels and other renewables. 53Wind crosses the threshold in 1978, solar in 1983 — both starting at essentially zero. 54The fourth revolution's fuels are wind, solar, and the AI-compute electricity demand they are racing to feed.

4th · 2010-present

69Solar + wind + AI compute

70Renewables go from 0.65% of primary energy (2010) to 6.05% (2024). 71Frontier AI training compute doubles every 5.7 months. 72The new fuel question is whether the grid can build out fast enough.

73How fossil never really lost share

74Vaclav Smil has been arguing for forty years that energy transitions take decades — that new sources reach a few percent of the market within a generation, but only displace incumbents over the next two or three. 75The post-1973 fossil-share trajectory is the cleanest expression of his thesis. Down from 7681.6% to 7776.4% 78in 51 years is real, but it is gentle.

79Inside the fossil block, coal lost share to oil and gas, but kept growing in absolute terms. 80Coal at 2024 = 8124.6% of primary energy, down from 55% in 1910 — yet 5x the volume burned in 1910. 83Oil went from 6 TWh in 1870 to 8455,292 TWh 85in 2024. 86Natural gas, the supposed transition fuel, sits at 8741,278 TWh 88and is still climbing. 89Each one displaced the rest in share only because the pie kept getting bigger.

The CO2 receipt

Annual world CO2 92emissions in 1750 were 939.3 million tonnes94, almost entirely from English coal. 95In 2024 they were 9638.6 billion tonnes — a multiplier of 974,148x. 98Every kink in the global energy mix shows up on the CO2 99curve, and only three modest declines are visible across the 274-year record: the 2008-09 financial crisis, a 2014-16 Chinese coal slowdown, and the pandemic dip in 2020. None of the nominal energy transitions — the rise of nuclear, the post-2010 renewables takeoff — register as a break.

101The intensity number does, slightly. Tonnes of CO2 102per TWh of non-biomass primary energy have fallen from 103345,954 104in 1850 to 105220,221 in 2024 — about a 36% decline over 174 years, or 0.3% per year. 107Oil and gas are cleaner than coal, and the renewables wedge is effectively zero. 108But the rate of intensity decline is far slower than what a true energy transition would require: getting the curve to zero needs either a 95%+ intensity cut or an absolute contraction of fossil volume, and the 21st century has so far delivered neither.

109Cumulative responsibility for those 1101.8 trillion tonnes 111of country-attributed CO2 112is even more concentrated than current flows suggest. 113The United States alone is 11424.1% 115of the historical total; China is 11615.8%117, despite producing more annually today than the US ever has. The next tier — Russia, Germany, the UK, Japan — are all early-industrial economies whose 19th- and 20th-century emissions still dominate. 119India is the world's third-largest current emitter but only seventh cumulatively, because its industrial era started so recently.

126Renewables are real, but the world wants more of everything

127Solar and wind together produced 12811 TWh of primary energy in 1990 — essentially a rounding error. 130In 2024 they produced 13111,275 TWh132, a thousand-fold increase across 34 years. 133The doubling has not slowed: 990 TWh (2010), 2,755 TWh (2015), 6,057 TWh (2020), 11,275 TWh (2024). 134Solar caught wind in 2024 and is now the world's fastest-deploying source of new electricity generation.

135But renewables are not the only thing growing. 136Per-capita energy demand is climbing almost everywhere outside the United States and Western Europe. China went from 1372,124 kWh 138per person in 1965 to 13934,514 in 2024 — sixteen-fold growth, narrowly crossing the EU average for the first time. India is at 1417,813 kWh 142per person, less than a quarter of the EU level. 143Africa has barely moved, from 1442,228 to 1454,055 kWh 146per person in 60 years.

147The benchmark to keep in mind: US per-capita energy in 1965 was 14873,802 kWh. 149China today consumes 47% of that. 150India 11%. 151Africa 5%. 152If the rest of the world reaches even half the US-1965 level by 2050, total global energy demand needs to roughly double again — on top of the renewables buildout. 153The IEA's most optimistic Renewables 2024 forecast has wind and solar PV reaching 30% of electricity generation by 2030. 154Electricity is roughly a quarter of total energy. The math is tight.

158AI compute is the new fuel demand

159Frontier AI training compute doubled every 16019.7 months 161from the start of machine learning in 1950 through 2009. 162From 2010 onwards, the doubling time collapsed to 1635.7 months — a 3.4x 164acceleration that lines up almost exactly with Epoch AI's published headline that post-2010 compute is doubling every six months. 165The 17 years since 2010 contain ten orders of magnitude of growth: the maximum-compute model in 2010 used 5.4×1016 FLOP. 166In 2025 it was 5×1026 FLOP — Grok 4, from xAI.

The median model has tracked along: from 1×1016 FLOP in 2010 to 3.0×1024 170FLOP in 2024, a hundred-million-fold increase in fourteen years. 171This is the empirical signature of the fourth industrial revolution as a compute revolution, distinct from but parallel to the renewables buildout. 172It is the most dramatic single-decade growth curve of any energy-consuming workload in human history.

173Where is the compute coming from? 174Not, mostly, from the chips themselves. Moore's Law — Intel 4004 to today's 58-billion-transistor chips, a 17625-million-fold density gain across 50 years — slowed to a 17826.5-month 179doubling time after 2010, a modest deceleration from the prior 18023.5 months. 181The post-2010 compute curve is roughly five times steeper than the per-chip transistor curve, which means the gap is being closed by parallelism and runtime: more chips deployed per training run, and longer runs.

195A single training run draws as much power as a town

196More chips and longer runs convert directly to more electricity. 197Maximum recorded training power draw for an AI model grew from 1982.1 kW 199in 2015 (small academic experiments) to 200109.9 MW 201in 2025. 202That is roughly a tenfold increase every four years, or doubling annually. 203110 MW sustained for weeks or months is in the same league as a steel mill or a mid-sized data center, but concentrated on a single workload.

204The 110 MW number belongs to a specific model: Grok 3 from xAI, published 17 February 2025, at 3.5×1026 FLOP. The other 100+ MW-scale runs in the database — Llama 4 Behemoth (44 MW), Gemini 1.0 Ultra (38 MW), Llama 3.1-405B (23 MW), GPT-4 (20 MW) — sit just below it. 207All are from the last three years. The very top of the compute table — Grok 4, GPT-4.5, Grok 3, GPT-5 — collectively burned roughly 20910,000 times 210the training compute of the original GPT-4.

211The IEA's 2025 Energy and AI report projects global data-centre electricity demand growing from 212~415 TWh 213in 2024 to 214~945 TWh by 2030 — from 1.5% of world electricity to just under 3%, in six years. 216Hyperscale AI campuses now routinely contract for 100 MW+ interconnections, with some planned at gigawatt scale. 217Most regional grids cannot supply this without 5-10 years of transmission upgrades that have not yet been built.

226Where the four revolutions leave us

227Each industrial revolution found a new fuel, scaled it, and left the previous ones in place. 228Coal did not retire when oil arrived. 229Oil did not retire when gas arrived. 230None of them retired when nuclear, solar, or wind arrived. 231The fourth industrial revolution is no different. 232Its signature fuel is electricity-on-demand for AI training compute, and the grid is being asked to build out for that workload at the same time it is being asked to absorb a renewables wedge that is already growing as fast as anything in industrial history.

233Smil's old observation holds. 234The wood-to-coal-to-oil-to-gas transition took more than a century. 235Solar and wind have done roughly a thousand-fold expansion in 34 years and now serve 6% of primary energy. 236AI training compute is doubling every 5.7 months. These three curves are not parallel — they are running on entirely different clocks. 238The interesting question for the next ten years is whether the build-out clock can outrun the demand clock.

239What this dataset cannot answer is whether the answer is yes. 240It can only show what the stack looks like so far: four revolutions, six primary fuels still in use, 76% fossil, 4,148x the CO2 241of 1750, and a new training run starting somewhere right now at 100-plus megawatts.
