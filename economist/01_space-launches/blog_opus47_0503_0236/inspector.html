2The Great Launch Inversion

3In 1982 the Soviet Union launched 108 rockets to orbit. 4By 2018 its successor managed ten — and the world's most prolific operator was a private American firm that did not exist when the Soviet Union dissolved.

5In 1982 the Soviet Union launched 108 rockets to orbit — more than four times what the United States managed that year. 6By 2018 the inheritor of that programme, Russia, had launched ten. 7The single largest launcher in 2018 was a state agency that did not exist when Sputnik flew, and the second largest was a private company that did not exist when the Soviet Union dissolved.

8Through October 2018, China conducted 28 orbital launches, the United States 27, the rest of the world 15, and Russia 10. 9Among individual operators, only SpaceX, with 17 launches, breaks past the United States' state count.

10If you froze the world in 1985 and asked who would dominate the launch business in 2018, almost no one would have answered "a private American company and the People's Republic of China." That is what happened.

The long arc

15Global launch cadence has never been steady. 16From three attempts in 1957, the world reached 139 launches in 1967 and stayed near that mark for two and a half decades — the high plateau of the Cold War. 17After 1991 the line falls off a cliff: the post-Soviet 1990s averaged 86 launches per year, and the floor came in 2004 with just 53.

18During the Cold War, the duopoly was almost total. 19Through 1991, the USSR and US between them accounted for 95.6% of every orbital launch — 2,444 and 1,055 respectively. 20China contributed 0.9%. 21Everyone else managed 3.5%.

22After 1991 the picture flattens dramatically. 23Russia carries forward 35.6% of the post-Soviet total, the United States 32.0%, "Other" rises to 19.4%, and China climbs to 13.0%. 24The recovery in the 2010s back toward 90 launches per year does not restore the old order — it builds a new one.

The bloc handover

29Strip the global total apart by bloc and the handover is dramatic. 30The Soviet line is the dominant feature of the chart from 1967 to 1991, peaking at 108 launches in 1982 — a cadence the Americans never reached in any single year. 31The American line wobbles around 20 in the 1980s as defence-launch demand collapsed. 32China crawls along the floor for thirty years before lifting off after 2007.

33By 2010 China is launching as many rockets per year as Russia. 34By 2018 it is launching more than anyone. 35The "Other" bloc — Europe, Japan, India, Israel, Iran, North Korea, two British and two Brazilian attempts — has thickened to a third strand of the picture, no longer the rounding error it was during the Cold War.

43From state to market

44Of the 5,726 launches in the data, 4,776 — 83.4% — were conducted by state agencies. 45Until 1984 the figure is 100%: every rocket above the Karman line was a government rocket. 46Private operators conducted 880 launches in total (15.4%). 47Startups, the smallest category at 1.2%, contributed 70.

48The cross-over comes early. 49By 2000, private operators were already launching 45 rockets a year against the state's 38. 50The state line stays roughly flat at around 40-50 a year for the rest of the dataset; the private and startup lines fight for the top half of every chart from 2000 onward.

53SpaceX in fast-forward

54SpaceX flew once in 2006. 55It did not reach 5 launches a year until 2014. 56Then 7. 57Then 8. 58Then 18 in 2017 — the year it ate the share previously owned by United Launch Alliance, Arianespace and Soyuz combined. 59By October 2018 it had flown 17 more.

60Plot SpaceX cumulatively against the cadence of the historical state programmes and the slope is the steepest line in the dataset. 61From a standing start in 2006 to 68 launches by October 2018, the curve doubles in length every two-to-three years. 62After the December 2015 Falcon 9 land-back, it doesn't bend — it bends harder.

64The "startup" category in the data is, in practice, two firms: SpaceX with 68 launches and Rocket Lab USA with 1. 65Together they made 37 launches in 2017 and 2018 — more than half of all startup launches in the dataset's 61-year span. 66The category is essentially a graph of one company's first decade.

Learning to land

69The dataset begins with rockets that fell out of the sky most of the time. 70Fifty-two attempts were made in 1957–1959; 28 of them failed — a 53.8% failure rate. 71The 1960s settled at 13%. 72The 1970s halved that to 5.1%. 73The 1980s halved it again, to 2.9%. 74Every decade since has stayed between 3% and 5%.

84A geography that has barely moved

85Every launch in the dataset traces back to one of seventeen national sponsors, but only six of them — the Soviet Union, the United States, Russia, China, France, and Japan — have ever launched more than a hundred times.

86The geography of these launches is remarkably stable. 87Baikonur, Plesetsk, Cape Canaveral, Vandenberg, Jiuquan, Xichang, Tanegashima, Kourou: the cluster of high-latitude or equatorial spaceports built between 1957 and 1972 has barely changed. 88New entrants — Wenchang in Hainan (China, 2016), Mahia Peninsula in New Zealand (Rocket Lab) — extend the map rather than redraw it. 89Kourou, in French Guiana, sits at 5°N and remains the most equatorial major spaceport in active use, giving European launchers a payload bonus that the Cold War's high-latitude pads cannot match.
