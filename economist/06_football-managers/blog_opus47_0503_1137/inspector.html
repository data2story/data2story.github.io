Fifteen seasons. Five leagues. 327,390 fixtures. 4One uncomfortable distribution.

5The miracle that wasn't a miracle

6Leicester City won the 2015-16 Premier League title beating their FIFA-rating-based forecast by 1.07 points per game — about 41 extra league points across the season, the most extreme single-season over-performance in 15 years of big-five-league data.

7But pull back to the full distribution of 502 manager-tenures of at least one full season, and Leicester is a curiosity, not the rule. 8The average manager added 0.04 points per game above what player skill predicted — statistically indistinguishable from luck. 9The 5th-to-95th-percentile range of overperformance runs only from -0.24 to +0.34 points per game. 10Almost half of all tenures sit within 0.10 of zero.

14A model the betting markets respect

15To know whether a manager is over- or under-performing, you first need to know what their squad ought to achieve. 16The Economist built a forecast using EA's FIFA video-game ratings — the only public skill estimate covering more than a decade — and ran it across 27,390 league fixtures from 2004 to 2019. 17Players were z-scored within position group, exponentiated to capture the non-linear gap between an elite and a merely good footballer, and fed into a per-league logistic regression that predicted home wins, draws and away wins.

18The result is a forecast that, on average, misses each team's seasonal points total by about 7.3 points across all 1,470 team-seasons in the sample. 19For comparison, preseason betting markets — the wisdom of millions of staked pounds — produce average errors only fractionally smaller, around 8 points for English campaigns. 20The correlation between expected and actual points-per-game across all team-seasons is 0.80. 21The benchmark is real.

25The leaderboard nobody expects

26Now sort every full-season-or-longer manager tenure by points-per-game above expectation. 27The leaderboard is dominated not by household names but by short, brilliant interruptions. 28Abelardo's 42-game stretch at Deportivo Alavés tops the list at +0.57 ppg. 29Ralph Hasenhüttl's 68 games at RB Leipzig follow at +0.55. 30Two Luciano Spalletti tenures, a Ralf Rangnick stint, a Felix Magath title at Wolfsburg — these are the names that out-performed.

The killer test

37The hardest question a coach's CV faces is whether their record at one club predicts their record at the next. 38Among 222 managers in this dataset who had at least two distinct tenures, the correlation between first-tenure and second-tenure points-per-game above expectation is 0.04 — statistically indistinguishable from no relationship at all (p = 0.54). 39Of managers who over-performed in tenure one, only 45% over-performed in tenure two. 40Of those who under-performed, 37% over-performed at the next club. 41Both numbers hover around a coin flip.

42Split the same managers into quartiles by their first-tenure result, and watch what happens at the next club. 43Every group, including the very top, collapses back toward zero. 44The bottom quartile (mean -0.4 ppg in tenure one) ended tenure two at -0.04. 45The top quartile (mean +0.3) ended at -0.001. 46The apparent good and bad coaches of round one converge in round two — regression to the mean in its purest visible form.

54Why a single season tells you almost nothing

8

55The Economist's modellers found, by optimising for forecast accuracy, that the best prediction of a manager's future impact requires adding 461 games of league-average performance to his actual record before drawing any conclusions. 56That is a brutal multiplier: a coach with a single 38-game season retains only 7.6% of his record after shrinkage. 57A three-season tenure retains a fifth. 58Ten seasons gets you to 45%. 59Twenty seasons of work — 760 games — reaches just 62%. 60Effectively, the data refuses to hear a manager's voice clearly until he has worked for two decades.

65This is what makes Sir Alex Ferguson exceptional in the technical sense. 66Ferguson logged 342 league games at Manchester United inside the data window, averaging 2.25 points per game versus 2.09 expected — only +0.159 above expectation, but he kept producing it for a quarter of a century. 67His tenure retains 43% of its weight after shrinkage. 68Most managers, the model says, simply do not exist long enough to be heard above the noise.

Klopp, the textbook

69Jürgen Klopp's career across three clubs reads like the textbook over-performance trajectory the rest of this analysis insists is rare. 70At Mainz 05 (2001-08, 102 covered games) he edged 0.05 ppg above expectation. 71At Borussia Dortmund (2008-15, 238 games) he leapt to +0.18 ppg — the spine of the original Economist piece's third chart. 72At Liverpool (from October 2015, 126 games covered) he rose again to +0.31. 73The line goes only one way, and each step rests on enough games to drown out luck.

76The famous-name table is not what you think

77The same lens makes other reputations look thinner. 78Pep Guardiola's three tenures at Barcelona, Bayern and Manchester City all post positive margins, but only modestly — +0.11, +0.08, +0.28. 79Diego Simeone is consistently elite at Atlético (+0.26 over 267 games). 80Antonio Conte's two Juventus stints are spectacular (+0.44, +0.38). 81Mauricio Pochettino climbed from -0.02 at Espanyol to +0.30 at Tottenham.

82Carlo Ancelotti is the surprise. 83Across his AC Milan, Chelsea, PSG, Real Madrid and Bayern tenures the model rates him between -0.27 and +0.03 ppg above expectation — a man with a cabinet of Champions League trophies, and on this metric a roughly average coach paired with a string of elite squads. 84The data is not telling you Ancelotti is bad. 85It is telling you that 86given the players he had87, an average coach would have done about as well.

109What this data does, and does not, say

16 11030 s · sparse instrumental underscore (optional)

111None of this proves managers do not matter. 112It proves that the variation between managers, given the players they actually inherit, is small relative to the variation that comes from squad quality alone. 113A few coaches — Ferguson, Klopp, Simeone, Conte at his peak — show up as outliers strong enough to clear regression to the mean. Most do not.

114What this challenges is the industry's reflexive logic of dismissal. 115Premier League clubs paid more than £90 million in manager severance in 2018-19 alone. 116The data here suggests that the new boss is, on the evidence, almost always indistinguishable from the old one. 117The post-sacking 'bounce' that fans cite is largely the team's underlying quality reasserting itself — exactly what would have happened anyway. 118Hiring and firing has become football's most expensive ritual aimed at one of its smallest variables.
