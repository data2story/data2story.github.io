Locked In, Priced Out

The Geography of a Prison Price

A packet of ramen. A stick of deodorant. A fan to survive the heat. What they cost depends almost entirely on which state you're locked up in.

Scroll ↓
The Spread

The Same Ramen, Four Times the Price

The same packet of ramen costs $0.24 in a North Carolina prison and $1.06 in Florida — more than four times as much for an identical product. For hygiene basics the gap is wider still: deodorant runs six cents in Ohio and $2.90 in Alaska, a 48-fold spread, and toothpaste stretches from $0.12 to $3.67.

4.4×ramen, $0.24 to $1.06
30.6×toothpaste, $0.12 to $3.67
48×deodorant, $0.06 to $2.90

This is not one strange outlier. Ranked across all 46 states that opened their books to The Appeal, ramen climbs in a smooth gradient from North Carolina's 24 cents to Florida's lone dollar-plus packet, with a national median of just 47 cents.

Outside prison, that same packet costs about 30 to 35 cents at a store like Target. Inside, where the commissary is the only shop in town, a captive market sets a price that can run three times higher.

The Wage

The Exchange Rate Is Human Labor

To understand these prices you have to know the wage behind them. Incarcerated workers earn roughly 14 to 63 cents an hour nationally — and far less in places like Louisiana, which averages six cents, or Texas, which pays nothing at all. Many work full days for about six dollars every two weeks.

At six cents an hour, the median deodorant costs 22 hours of work. Toothpaste, 21 hours. A jar of peanut butter, 42 hours. The numbers stop reading like prices and start reading like sentences.

And an electric fan — not a luxury, but often the only defense against dangerous heat in prisons without air conditioning — costs 382 hours at that wage. That is more than nine full forty-hour weeks of labor for a single fan.

The Heat

The Price of Staying Cool

A fan should be the cheapest insurance against a heat wave. In prison it is a financial event. The cheapest fan ranges from $10.02 in Wyoming to $43.80 in Nevada — the same 4.4x spread we saw with ramen, only now the stakes are heatstroke.

Nine states price their cheapest fan above $27; South Dakota, Indiana, Utah, and Georgia all clear $32. The Appeal documented fans selling for nearly $40 at one Delaware facility and around $33 in Indiana, in buildings that routinely bake through the summer.

Cheapest electric fan, by state (30 states that list one). A 4.4x spread from Wyoming to Nevada. Source: The Appeal.
The Turn

What Costs the Most Is Faith

Rank all 24 products by price and the cheapest things are the ones a prison cannot really withhold: a toothbrush at 42 cents, ramen at 47, soap at 66. The most expensive things are the items tied to faith — and a fan.

$25.99 vs $5.21 median price of a Quran versus a Bible — nearly five times more for the central text of Islam

A Quran has a median price of $25.99; a Bible, $5.21 — nearly five times less for the central text of Christianity than for the central text of Islam. Connecticut's list showed it starkly: a Bible for $4.55, the Noble Quran for $25.99. The Star of David and the crucifix both run above $25, while Christian rosary beads cost under two dollars.

Prayer rugs ($15.77 median) and hijabs ($12.27) pile further cost onto Islamic practice. Religious access is a protected right, but in the commissary the financial barrier to practicing it falls hardest on Muslims and Jews.

Median cheapest price across states, by product type. Faith items and the electric fan top the list; the staples a prison must let people keep clean and fed sit at the bottom. Some religious items have small samples (Quran, n=5), so read those as indicative. Source: The Appeal.
The Market

Who Sets the Price

So who decides what a fan or a Quran costs? Of the 46 states, 17 run their commissaries themselves, but 29 hand the store to a private vendor. Keefe Commissary alone supplies 16 states. These contracts typically pay the state a commission on every sale — so the prison earns more when the prices go up, not down.

Number of states served by each commissary operator. State-run (17) and Keefe Commissary (16) dominate. Source: The Appeal.

The result is a geography of cost. The same basket of needs averages $8.36 per item in Indiana and just $0.93 in Michigan — nine times more in one state than another. Where a person is incarcerated, far more than what they buy, decides what survival costs.
