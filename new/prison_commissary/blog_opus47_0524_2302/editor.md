## Story Spine
**Core claim**: In America's prisons, the price of a packet of ramen, a stick of deodorant, or a fan to survive the heat depends almost entirely on which state you happen to be locked up in — and against wages of pennies an hour, that lottery decides what basic dignity costs.

**Tension**: We assume a national product has a roughly national price. Inside prison, the same ramen costs four times more in Florida than North Carolina, deodorant costs forty-eight times more in one state than another, and the people paying earn six cents an hour.

**Payoff**: The reader stops seeing a commissary receipt as a list of small numbers and starts seeing it as hours — sometimes weeks — of a human being's labor, set by geography and a captive market.

## Sections

### edt_01: Hook — The same ramen, four times the price
**Evidence**: ana_01, ana_02 | **Context**: det_01, det_03

[ana_01] The same packet of ramen costs $0.24 in a North Carolina prison and $1.06 in Florida — more than four times as much for an identical product. For hygiene basics the gap is wider still: deodorant runs six cents in Ohio and $2.90 in Alaska, a 48-fold spread, and toothpaste stretches from $0.12 to $3.67.

[ana_02, det_01] This is not one strange outlier. Ranked across all 46 states that opened their books to The Appeal, ramen climbs in a smooth gradient from North Carolina's 24 cents to Florida's lone dollar-plus packet, with a national median of just 47 cents.

[det_03] Outside prison, that same packet costs about 30 to 35 cents at a store like Target. Inside, where the commissary is the only shop in town, a captive market sets a price that can run three times higher.

### edt_02: The exchange rate is human labor
**Evidence**: ana_03 | **Context**: det_02

[det_02] To understand these prices you have to know the wage behind them. Incarcerated workers earn roughly 14 to 63 cents an hour nationally — and far less in places like Louisiana, which averages six cents, or Texas, which pays nothing at all. Many work full days for about six dollars every two weeks.

[ana_03] At six cents an hour, the median deodorant costs 22 hours of work. Toothpaste, 21 hours. A jar of peanut butter, 42 hours. The numbers stop reading like prices and start reading like sentences.

[ana_03] And an electric fan — not a luxury, but often the only defense against dangerous heat in prisons without air conditioning — costs 382 hours at that wage. That is more than nine full forty-hour weeks of labor for a single fan.

### edt_03: The price of staying cool
**Evidence**: ana_07 | **Context**: det_06

[ana_07] A fan should be the cheapest insurance against a heat wave. In prison it is a financial event. The cheapest fan ranges from $10.02 in Wyoming to $43.80 in Nevada — the same 4.4x spread we saw with ramen, only now the stakes are heatstroke.

[ana_07, det_06] Nine states price their cheapest fan above $27; South Dakota, Indiana, Utah, and Georgia all clear $32. The Appeal documented fans selling for nearly $40 at one Delaware facility and around $33 in Indiana, in buildings that routinely bake through the summer.

### edt_04: What costs the most is faith
**Evidence**: ana_04, ana_05 | **Context**: det_05

[ana_04] Rank all 24 products by price and the cheapest things are the ones a prison cannot really withhold: a toothbrush at 42 cents, ramen at 47, soap at 66. The most expensive things are the items tied to faith — and a fan.

[ana_05, det_05] A Quran has a median price of $25.99; a Bible, $5.21 — nearly five times less for the central text of Christianity than for the central text of Islam. Connecticut's list showed it starkly: a Bible for $4.55, the Noble Quran for $25.99. The Star of David and the crucifix both run above $25, while Christian rosary beads cost under two dollars.

[ana_05] Prayer rugs ($15.77 median) and hijabs ($12.27) pile further cost onto Islamic practice. Religious access is a protected right, but in the commissary the financial barrier to practicing it falls hardest on Muslims and Jews.

[CHART: ana_04]

### edt_05: Who sets the price
**Evidence**: ana_06, ana_08 | **Context**: det_04

[ana_06, det_04] So who decides what a fan or a Quran costs? Of the 46 states, 17 run their commissaries themselves, but 29 hand the store to a private vendor. Keefe Commissary alone supplies 16 states. These contracts typically pay the state a commission on every sale — so the prison earns more when the prices go up, not down.

[ana_08] The result is a geography of cost. The same basket of needs averages $8.36 per item in Indiana and just $0.93 in Michigan — nine times more in one state than another. Where a person is incarcerated, far more than what they buy, decides what survival costs.

## Editorial Notes
- Exact numbers that must not be rounded or altered: ramen $0.24 / $1.06 (4.4x), deodorant $0.06 / $2.90 (48x), fan $10.02 / $43.80, 382 hours at $0.06/hr, Quran $25.99 vs Bible $5.21, Michigan $0.93 vs Indiana $8.36.
- Caveat that must stay visible somewhere: this is a 46-of-50-state snapshot; Alabama, Louisiana, New York, and Tennessee did not provide lists (weave into edt_01 chart caption or references). Wage figures are from external reporting, not the dataset (det_02) — keep the "roughly" framing.
- Load-bearing sections: edt_01 (hook/lead) and edt_02 (the labor exchange rate) are the spine. edt_03, edt_04, edt_05 each add one distinct dimension (heat, faith, who profits) and should not be cut.
- Religious small-sample caveat (ana_caveat_03) should be acknowledged lightly in edt_04 — use medians, present as indicative.
