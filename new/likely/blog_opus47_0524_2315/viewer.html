How likely is ‘likely’?

6The same word can mean almost anything. 7The disagreement lives between us, not inside us.

In 1951 a US intelligence report warned of a “serious possibility” that the Soviet Union would attack Yugoslavia. 10The CIA analyst Sherman Kent, curious, asked his colleagues what “serious possibility” meant. 11Their answers ranged from a 20% chance to an 80% chance — for the very same three words in the very same sentence.

12Seventy-five years later, 5,174 people took an online quiz and put a number on “realistic possibility.” British intelligence officially defines it as 40–50%. 13Only 17.5% of people landed in that band. Most — 64% — read it as better than even, with a mean of 57% and answers smeared across the entire range from 0 to 100. The word a government uses to mean “a bit less than even” is heard by most people as “probably.”

19Line the 19 phrases up by their average and you get a reassuringly sensible ladder. “Will Happen” sits at the top (mean 98%), then “Almost Certain” (94%), “Highly Likely” (85%), down through “Likely” (73%) and “Probable” (71%), past “About Even” (exactly 50%), all the way to “Almost No Chance” at the bottom (mean 4%, median 2%).

21It looks like a shared scale. It isn’t. The average is a magician’s trick — it hides exactly how much we disagree about each rung.

25Where consensus collapses

26Where consensus collapses

27Rank the same phrases by how much people disagree — the spread of their answers — and a different story appears. “Realistic Possibility” is the most contested word in the language tested here, with answers spanning 35 points between the typical low and high guess. The hedging verbs “Could Happen,” “Might Happen” and “May Happen” follow close behind.

30Agreement only returns at the extremes. “About Even,” “Almost Certain,” “Almost No Chance” and “Will Happen” are the tightest. 32Disagreement peaks in the murky middle and around hedge words, and vanishes where words are absolute — the same shape every formal study since Kent has found, from a NATO panel of 23 officers onward.

The title fight

35The title fight: likely vs probable

The quiz’s namesake question — does “likely” beat “probable”? — is almost a coin flip. When shown the pair head-to-head, 53% picked “likely.” Other matchups are even closer: “Improbable” vs “Remote Chance” and “Could Happen” vs “Might Happen” both split 50.8 to 49.2.

39Stranger still, the answer depends on how you ask. By average value, “Likely” edges out “Probable” (73 vs 71). But by head-to-head win rate, “Probable” wins more of its fights (78% vs 69%) — so the two methods rank them in opposite order. 42Where two words mean nearly the same thing, the measurement method itself can crown a different winner.

    It’s not you, it’s us

    It’s not you, it’s us

    54Here is the twist that matters most. All this disagreement might just be noise — people guessing differently each time. It isn’t. 56Every respondent was secretly shown one pair of phrases twice. 57When it came back around, 96.6% chose the same winner both times. 58Only 3.4% flipped.

    8 5996.6% 60of people gave the same answer when secretly shown the same pair twice — the disagreement is between us, not within us

    61So we are each remarkably consistent with ourselves. We hold a firm private sense of what “likely” means. 63The problem is that your firm private meaning and mine simply don’t match — and neither of us can tell, because the word sounds identical coming out of both our mouths.

    64The shortcuts in our heads

    65The shortcuts in our heads

    We also don’t answer like instruments. More than half of all 98,306 estimates — 53.6% — were exact multiples of ten, and another 31.2% ended in five. 68That leaves barely 15% of answers using any other digit. The number “4” shows up in just one of every two hundred responses.

    About one in five people — 18.8% — gave answers that were all multiples of five, every single phrase. We aren’t reading a probability off a mental ruler; we’re picking from a short menu of tidy numbers, which quietly compresses the real differences between words.

    The explanations that don’t explain

    The explanations that don’t explain

    78Surely some groups read these words differently? A little. Americans are the most “optimistic” readers, sitting about half a point above the global average; Britons fall just below it; Norwegians are the most cautious. 80On raw numbers, Americans average 46.2% to the British 45.1% — a ratio of 1.02. 81But the entire spread from the most to least optimistic country is under three percentage points.

    82Age barely registers, and the order the phrases were shown in moved answers by less than a single point across all nineteen positions. The tidy demographic stories we’d reach for to explain the disagreement don’t carry it. The variation isn’t between nations or generations — it’s between individual minds, and it’s stubborn.

    Say the number

    Say the number

    89Estimative words run through the decisions that shape lives — intelligence assessments, IPCC climate reports, medical consent forms, weather forecasts, financial disclosures. When “likely” means 55% to one reader and 90% to another, the same sentence can justify opposite choices.

    91So here is the practical lesson buried in 150,000 judgements: if the number matters, say the number. The word “likely” will not carry it for you.
