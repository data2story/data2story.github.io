4The Most Prolific Stanford Founder Went to Stanford for Two Days

5The most prolific founder to come out of Stanford is Elon Musk, with fifteen organizations to his name. 6He also attended Stanford for about two days, in 1995, before walking out of a physics PhD to go chase the internet.

7That single fact is the whole story of this dataset in miniature. 8Stanford really is a founder factory — but the records that prove it are built on words like "founded" and "educated" that turn out to be far looser than they sound.

The raw material is 545 founder→organization links pulled from Wikidata: 394 distinct Stanford-educated people credited with starting 509 different organizations across 149 industries. 10It is the spine of Silicon Valley, captured as a spreadsheet.

13A Curve Shaped Like Silicon Valley

14Lay the foundings out by decade and you get the rise of Silicon Valley as a single line. A trickle before the war — three organizations in the 1910s — climbs to eighteen in the 1960s, fifty in the 1980s, seventy-three in the 1990s, and peaks at a hundred and twenty in the 2000s.

16None of this was an accident. 17In the 1930s Stanford's engineering dean, Frederick Terman, started pushing his students to build companies near campus instead of leaving for the East Coast. 18He leased university land to high-tech firms and earned the title "father of Silicon Valley." The curve is the shape of his strategy paying off.

19By 2011, a Stanford survey counted nearly forty thousand active alumni-founded companies generating $2.7 trillion a year — output on the scale of the world's tenth-largest economy. 20This dataset is a tiny, famous slice of that machine.

22From a Garage to Palantir

23The list reads like the table of contents of a Silicon Valley history. It opens with Hewlett-Packard in 1939 — two Stanford students, a rented Palo Alto garage, the building now stamped "Birthplace of Silicon Valley."

25Then the 1980s wave: Silicon Graphics and Logitech in 1981, Sun Microsystems in 1982, Cisco in 1984. 26Sun's name was literally a pun on "Stanford University Network," and its first machine was built from spare parts in the campus computer lab. 27Cisco's router software descended from code written at Stanford. 28These companies weren't founded by alumni who happened to study there — they were spun straight out of the labs.

29From there it runs through Nvidia in 1993 and the dot-com duo of PayPal and Google in 1998, into LinkedIn and Palantir in the 2000s. 30Every name a household word, every one a single row in the same file.

31And Stanford rarely supplies just one founder per company. 32Thirty organizations here have more than one Stanford-educated founder — four at OpenAI, three apiece at Sun, Palantir, and PayPal. 33The factory ships teams, not just individuals.

37Most of the Records Can't Tell You What They Are

5 59% 38of records don't say what the company even does (323 of 545)

39Here is where the clean story gets honest. The dataset has a tidy "is technology" flag — but it is built on Wikidata's industry field, and 323 of the 545 records, fifty-nine percent, have no industry recorded at all. 41Only 104 links carry the tech flag. 42The flag is a floor, not a count: the famous tech firms sitting in that 59% fog are simply invisible to it.

43That fog distorts everything downstream. 44The tech share per decade looks like it collapses to zero in the 1960s and 1970s — not because Stanford stopped making tech companies, but because almost none of those older records have an industry filled in. 45Where the data exists, tech holds a steady twenty to thirty-five percent.

48Clear the Fog and Tech Wins

So clear the fog. 49Look only at the 202 organizations that actually have an industry recorded, and technology becomes the single largest bloc: eighty-eight of them, almost forty-four percent, are tech — more than every other industry combined.

50And when you list those named industries, they read like Silicon Valley's own glossary: software, computer hardware, artificial intelligence, information technology, the Internet, semiconductors. 51The founder-factory thesis survives contact with the messy data — you just have to know which numbers are a floor and which are the signal.

54The Factory Has an Address

55Map the headquarters and Terman's strategy is still visible on the ground. 56Of the companies with a known location, they pile up within a few miles of campus: San Francisco, Mountain View, Palo Alto, Menlo Park, Sunnyvale, San Jose. New York and Washington trail far behind — and Washington's count is mostly Herbert Hoover's relief commissions, not startups.

58Eighty-odd years after a dean told his students to stay close to home, the map of what they built still clusters around the same handful of Peninsula towns. 59The factory has an address.
