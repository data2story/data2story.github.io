## Story Spine
**Core claim**: The Forbes AI 50 looks like a diverse field of fifty companies, but read by the numbers it is really two stories stacked — a couple of foundation-model giants that hold almost all the money, and four dozen young, applied startups crowded into one California city.
**Tension**: We talk about "the AI industry" as if it were broad and many-headed. The data shows breadth in what these companies *do* and astonishing narrowness in where the money and the addresses actually are.
**Payoff**: The reader stops seeing the AI 50 as a leaderboard of equals and starts reading it as a map of concentration — of capital at the model frontier, of companies in the Bay Area, and of founding dates in the last three years.

## Sections

### edt_01: Hook — Guess who holds the money
**Evidence**: ana_02, ana_03 | **Context**: det_03, det_06

[ana_02] Add up every dollar raised by the fifty most promising private AI companies in the world and you get about $305.7 billion. Then notice that two of them — OpenAI and Anthropic — hold $242.6 billion of it. Two companies. 79.4% of the money.

[ana_03] The other forty-eight split what's left. The median company on this list has raised $735 million; the average has raised $6.1 billion. When the mean is more than eight times the median, you are not looking at a leaderboard — you are looking at a power law.

[det_03, editorial] This is what an AI gold rush looks like from the capital side: a vanishingly small number of foundation-model labs absorbing money at a scale venture capital has never seen, while everyone else operates one or two orders of magnitude below them.

### edt_02: The two giants and the long tail
**Evidence**: ana_02 | **Context**: det_03

[ana_02] Plot the full leaderboard and the shape is brutal. OpenAI's $182.6 billion bar dwarfs the page. Anthropic's $60 billion is the only thing visible next to it. Databricks at $20 billion is a distant third, and then the list collapses into a long, flat tail where Cursor's $3.3 billion — a number that would be a blockbuster anywhere else — barely registers.

[ana_03] Sixty-four percent of the list has raised under $1 billion. Only eighteen companies have crossed that mark at all. The two leaders alone outweigh the other forty-eight combined, four times over.

### edt_03: One city, half the list
**Evidence**: ana_05, ana_04 | **Context**: det_04

[ana_05] If the money is concentrated, the geography is almost comic. Exactly half the entire list — 25 of 50 companies — is headquartered in the city of San Francisco. Not the Bay Area, not California: the city itself.

[ana_05] Widen to the rest of California and you reach 33 of 50, two-thirds of the list. Only eleven companies are anywhere else in the United States, and just six are international. One city holds as many AI-50 companies as the rest of the world and the rest of America put together.

[ana_04, det_04] The international tail is thin enough to name in a breath: Cohere in Toronto, Mistral in Paris, Black Forest Labs in Freiburg, Synthesia in London, and two Swedish companies — Legora and Lovable — both in Stockholm. No company on the list is headquartered in Asia.

### edt_04: Born yesterday
**Evidence**: ana_06, ana_10 | **Context**: det_02, det_06

[ana_06] These are not seasoned institutions. Sixty-eight percent of the list was founded in 2020 or later, and 56% in 2022 or later — after ChatGPT's late-2022 debut lit the fuse. The single busiest founding year is 2022 with eleven companies, trailed by 2023 with ten. Those two years alone produced 21 of the 50.

[ana_06] The oldest company here, Databricks, dates to 2013. The newest, Thinking Machines Lab, was founded in 2025 — and had already raised $2 billion in time to make the list.

[ana_10] Age buys you nothing in this market. The correlation between founding year and funding is slightly negative, and the 2022-2025 cohort's median raise ($668 million) sits within reach of companies twice their age. Capital chases perceived frontier potential, not seniority.

### edt_05: A broad field, a narrow purse
**Evidence**: ana_07, ana_08 | **Context**: det_02, det_05, det_08

[ana_07] By headcount, the AI 50 is mostly applied AI, not labs. The largest single category is generative media — eight companies turning text into images, video, voice and music. Foundation models and AI infrastructure tie for second at seven each. Then come agents, coding tools, robotics, healthcare, legal, and data labeling.

[ana_09] The descriptions themselves carry the 2025 zeitgeist: five companies are built around "agents," eight around "generation," six around coding and app-building. The verbs have shifted from answering to doing.

[ana_08] But sort by dollars instead of by count and the field inverts. Those seven foundation-model companies — just 14% of the list — hold 83.2% of all the capital. Every other category combined, all the generative-media darlings and agent startups and coding tools, splits the remaining 16.8% — most of which is Databricks' $20 billion sitting by itself in infrastructure. The list is broad in what it builds and narrow in what it's worth.

### edt_06: Fifty companies, fifty faces
**Evidence**: ana_07, ana_11 | **Context**: det_08, det_09

[ana_11] Behind every row is a real company with a real logo. Eighteen of them appear here as verified marks pulled from Wikimedia Commons — OpenAI, Anthropic, Cohere, Mistral, Cursor, Perplexity, Runway, ElevenLabs, Suno and more — each credited to its source.

[ana_11, editorial] The rest wear clean typographic wordmarks, because honesty matters more than decoration: a dozen "logos" in the cache turned out to be impostors — Clay Telecom standing in for the AI startup Clay, the musician PJ Harvey for the legal-AI company Harvey, a piano-tutor app for the video company Synthesia — and inventing a logo for a company is worse than not showing one.

[ana_07] Browse them by category and the shape of the list becomes tangible: the model labs, the media generators, the agent-builders, the robot-makers, each a card you can pick up.

### edt_07: What the list can't tell you
**Evidence**: ana_01, ana_02 | **Context**: det_01, det_07

[det_01, det_07] Read all of this as a careful sketch, not a ledger. The AI 50 is a curated editorial list of private companies only — chosen by Forbes with Sequoia and Meritech — so it omits every public AI leader and any private company the algorithm didn't surface. Nvidia, Google and Microsoft are nowhere on it by design.

[ana_01, ana_02] The funding figures are rounded and frozen in time: Anthropic's "$60 billion" was already climbing past $180 billion in valuation within months, and two companies — Midjourney and Surge AI — read "$0" not because they have no money but because they never disclosed it or never needed it.

[editorial] So the numbers are a snapshot of a wave mid-break: the concentration is real, the youth is real, the California gravity is real. But a list this top-heavy and this recent is, above all, a bet on a future that hadn't arrived yet when the data was frozen.
