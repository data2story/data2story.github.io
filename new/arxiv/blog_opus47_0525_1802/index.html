arXiv, 1991–2025

A physics server that isn’t physics anymore

Built in 1991 for a handful of high-energy theorists, arXiv has quietly become a computer-science archive — and in one month of 2025, CS crossed half of everything posted.

The hook

A physics server that isn’t physics anymore

In May 2025, computer science accounted for 50.8% of everything posted to arXiv — the first time a single field broke half of the world’s largest preprint server in a month. As recently as January 2022 that share was 33.8%. In three and a half years, CS went from a third of arXiv to a majority.

Zoom out to the whole 2022–2025 window and the picture is just as lopsided: the computer-science archive holds 667,050 submissions, 42.5% of everything, which is 2.65 times the size of second-place mathematics. CS alone now produces more papers than the next three fields — math, condensed-matter physics, and physics — combined.

2.65× The CS archive is 2.65× larger than #2 mathematics — and bigger than the next three fields combined (667,050 submissions, 42.5% of all arXiv, 2022–2025).

Here is the twist. arXiv was not built for any of this. Physicist Paul Ginsparg launched it on August 14, 1991 as an automated email server so a small community of high-energy theorists could swap preprints; he expected maybe 100 papers a year. The server that now overflows with machine-learning research was designed for the exact opposite of a popular field.

The origin it left behind

From two papers to three million

arXiv’s first month, July 1991, held two papers. Ginsparg built the prototype with simple shell scripts on a NeXT machine; within six months it had taken 400 submissions instead of the trickle he planned for. The server moved with him to Cornell in 2001 and still runs there today on a famously modest budget.

From those two papers, output climbed almost monotonically for three decades: 3,263 submissions in the first full year of 1992, 30,601 by 2000, 178,329 by 2020, and 284,486 in 2025. Cumulatively arXiv crossed one million papers in December 2014, two million in January 2022, and stands at roughly 3.05 million today. A single month in 2025 now holds more papers than arXiv’s entire first four years.

Annual arXiv submissions, 1991–2025 (1991 partial — it began with 2 papers in July). Pins mark the cumulative crossings of 1M (Dec 2014) and 2M (Jan 2022). A single 2025 month now exceeds arXiv’s entire first four years. Source: ana_01.

That arc is the backdrop. The question is not whether arXiv grew — every research archive grows — but what it grew into.

Evidence

The four categories that ate arXiv

The takeover has four faces. Of 155 subject categories, the top four are all artificial intelligence: cs.LG (Machine Learning) is the single largest category on arXiv at 134,903 submissions, followed by cs.AI (101,916), cs.CV (Computer Vision, 100,238), and cs.CL (NLP and large language models, 61,144). The first non-CS category, quantum physics, sits at #5 with less than half of cs.CV’s count.

Top 20 of 155 categories by submissions, Jan 2022–Sep 2025. The four AI categories (cs.LG, cs.AI, cs.CV, cs.CL) glow in blue and dominate the top; the first non-CS category, quant-ph (#5), sits below half of cs.CV. Those four alone are 25.3% of all of arXiv. Source: ana_10/ana_11.

Together those four categories are 25.3% of everything posted — more than a quarter of all of arXiv from a single corner of computer science. The distribution is brutally top-heavy: the top 10 of 155 categories account for 37.2% of submissions, and just the top 20 cover more than half. A handful of fast-moving AI fields now sit on top of a very long tail of small specialties.

The cause is no mystery. The Transformer architecture arrived in a June 2017 arXiv preprint, “Attention Is All You Need,” and became the backbone of large language models. ChatGPT’s November 2022 launch detonated the gold rush. Machine learning had also developed an “arXiv-first” culture — results get posted the moment they are ready, often before any journal — so the field’s entire output flows straight into these counts.

The turn

Not growth — a takeover

It would be easy to assume every field is simply booming together. It isn’t. Between 2022 and the trailing year ending September 2025, CS rose from 36.7% to 46.7% of all submissions — up nearly 10 points — while physics fell from 34.9% to 28.9% and math from 17.7% to 14.6%. Yet physics and math both still grew about 20% in absolute terms. They didn’t shrink; CS just grew so much faster that everyone else lost share. CS ate the growth.

Each group’s share of all submissions, 2022 vs the trailing 12 months (ending Sep 2025). CS’s band widens from 36.7% to 46.7% while physics and math narrow — but hover any band: physics still grew 1.21× and math 1.20× in volume. They lost share, not size. Source: ana_15/ana_14.

Rank every category by growth and the entire leaderboard is computer science: cs.ET (Emerging Tech) tripled at 3.29x, cs.AI 2.87x, cs.CL 2.65x, cs.HC 2.60x, even robotics nearly doubled. At the other end sit the classical physics fields arXiv was built for, essentially frozen: accelerator physics actually shrank 6%, and high-energy experiment, high-energy phenomenology and information theory all grew under 10% in nearly four years. AI subfields tripling while the founding disciplines hold flat — that is the shape of a takeover, not a tide.

Evidence

How fast it happened

The steepness is the story. Comparing 2022 to the trailing year ending September 2025, cs.AI grew 2.87x and cs.CL 2.65x. Month to month it is sharper still: cs.CL went from 451 submissions in January 2022 to 2,208 in September 2025, and cs.AI from 908 to 4,248 — both roughly fivefold in under four years. These curves don’t drift upward; they bend.

The whole-archive numbers bend with them. arXiv first broke 20,000 submissions in a single month in May 2023 — about six months after ChatGPT launched — and its biggest months are all clustered in 2024 and 2025, peaking at 26,646 in September 2025. The AI surge didn’t just add a new field; it reset arXiv’s record book.

Evidence

Readers came too

Readers showed up too. Annual full-text downloads rose from 128,204 in 1994 to 334.6 million in 2020 and 410.1 million in 2025 — roughly a 3,000-fold climb, now averaging about 34 million downloads a month. (The download records have a 13-month gap that swallows all of 2024, so that year is simply missing rather than a collapse.)

The downloads line is deliberately broken across 2024 — no data, not a decline. The source file jumps from Nov 2023 straight to Jan 2025.
Annual downloads (blue, left) against annual submissions (amber, right), 1994–2025. Supply and demand move in near-lockstep (r = 0.94), but demand pulled ahead: reads per new paper rose 299 (2000) → 569 (2010) → 1,889 (2020). Source: ana_05/ana_06.

Supply and demand move almost in lockstep — monthly submissions and downloads correlate at r = 0.94 — but demand has pulled ahead. Each new paper drew about 299 downloads in 2000, 569 in 2010, and 1,889 by 2020. Every paper now commands far more attention than papers did two decades ago, as a global readership keeps mining a compounding back catalogue.

The close

Still climbing

If you expected the AI wave to be cresting, the data says otherwise. Through the last complete month, the CS archive grew 21.7% year-over-year (Jan–Sep 2024 versus Jan–Sep 2025), well above arXiv’s overall pace. There is no slowdown visible — yet.

By late 2025 — after the AI-slop crackdown — is CS finally slowing down?
2025-10 is a partial extraction, not real data — every archive drops ~95% in that single month, a data cutoff, not a research crash. That is why the chart stops at September.

That “yet” matters. In early 2025 arXiv was hit by a wave of LLM-generated “slop,” pushing moderator rejection rates from the historical ~4% to 10–12%, and in October 2025 it stopped accepting computer-science review and position papers unless they had already passed peer review. Whether that crackdown bent the curve is something the per-category data cannot show: those records end at a partial October 2025 — a truncated extraction where every field drops about 95% in a single month, which is a data cutoff, not a research crash.

But the overall curve does reach past that edge. The full-history monthly series runs to May 2026, and it shows the climb continuing: arXiv recorded its first-ever 30,000-submission month in March 2026 — 30,045 papers, an all-time high — with total output up 16.0% year-over-year across the January–May window. The crackdown may have reshaped what counts as a CS submission, but it has not bent the overall curve downward. Not yet.

Faced with that record volume, arXiv changed who is allowed in. On January 21, 2026 it stopped treating an institutional email address — for years the de facto key to the archive — as enough to endorse a first-time submitter. New authors now need either prior authorship on an accepted paper in the same domain, or a personal endorsement from an established arXiv author. The reason it gave was blunt: “an unsustainable increase in the number of non-scientific submissions, increasing the rejection rate and requiring excessive moderation and staff effort.” The tightening was piloted on Mathematics in December 2025, then rolled out to every category.

The gate has teeth. On top of the October 2025 rule that computer-science reviews and position papers must already have passed peer review, arXiv set a penalty for the worst cases: where there is incontrovertible evidence that authors never checked their LLM’s output, the submitter is banned for a year — and afterward must get work accepted at a peer-reviewed venue before arXiv will take it again. The bar for a “serious” submission, once just a working email, is now a track record.

So arXiv ends this story exactly where it stopped being a physics server: as the live record of a field that is still posting faster than anyone can moderate — and, for the first time, actively deciding who gets to post. The question the next year of data will answer is not whether AI took over arXiv — it already has — but whether arXiv can stay the place where AI gets written down.
