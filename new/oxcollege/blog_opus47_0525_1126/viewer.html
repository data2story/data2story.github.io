5The unwinnable question

6Which is Oxford's oldest college?

7Ask which Oxford college is the oldest and you have walked into an 800-year-old argument with no winner. 8Three colleges claim the title, and the data shows why nobody can settle it: University College (1249), Balliol (1263) and Merton (1264) are separated by just fifteen years.

9Each rests its claim on a different definition of "oldest." University College points to the earliest endowment, money left in William of Durham's 1249 will. 10Balliol claims the longest unbroken presence on a single site, its Broad Street home since around 1263. 11Merton holds the first formal statutes, the written constitution that made it self-governing in 1264.

26The shape of 800 years

27Two waves, seven centuries apart

28Plot every founding by century and Oxford's history stops looking continuous. 29Instead of steady growth there are two peaks: a medieval wave in the 13th and 14th centuries, and a modern wave in the 19th and 20th — nine colleges in each of the last two, the joint-largest centuries on record.

38Between the two waves sits a long, quiet middle — the part of the story most people never picture when they imagine an ancient university.

The trough

39The Georgian silence

40After the Tudor century, Oxford essentially stopped. 41The single largest gap in the entire founding record runs 156 years, from 1714 to 1870 — and the eighteenth century contributes exactly one college, Worcester. 42The other long gaps are real but smaller: seventy-five years, then fifty-one, then a pair of forty-eights deep in the medieval period.

6
43The five largest gaps between consecutive founding years. 44The 156-year Georgian silence is roughly double the next-longest.

45The quiet middle of the barbell is not a gentle slowdown. 46It is a near-total halt — broken only when the Victorians arrived with new ideas about who a university was for.

47Destruction dressed as growth

48The Reformation churn

49The sixteenth century looks like a boom — eight colleges, the second-busiest century — but much of it is destruction wearing growth's clothes. 50Henry VIII's Reformation dissolved the monastic colleges: Gloucester fell in 1539, St Mary's in 1540.

51Out of the rubble rose new foundations on the same stone. 52Cardinal Wolsey's Cardinal College was suppressed when Wolsey fell, refounded as King Henry VIII's College, and finally re-established by the king in 1546 as Christ Church. 53All three names survive as separate rows — one institution counted three times.

57This is why the dataset mixes the living and the dead. 58Eleven of its fifty rows are defunct, dissolved, merged, or not residential colleges at all. 59Strip them out and you are left with the roughly thirty-nine colleges that exist today.

62When the silence broke

63The women's colleges arrive

64When the silence broke, it broke for women. 65The Victorian spike of 1850–1899 is largely the arrival of women's colleges: Lady Margaret Hall in 1878, Somerville the next year, then St Anne's, St Hugh's and St Hilda's by 1893.

69One founder appears twice in this wave. 70Elizabeth Wordsworth founded both Lady Margaret Hall and St Hugh's — arguably the most productive single founder in the dataset, since the only other repeat founders, Wolsey and Henry VIII, were really renaming the same Reformation college.

71The catch is that these institutions existed for decades before their students could graduate. 72Women attended lectures and sat exams but were not admitted to full Oxford degrees until 1920. 73The dataset records the building forty-two years before it records the right.

Reading the names

74From saints to surnames

75Read the namesake column top to bottom and you watch England secularise. 76Of the thirty-seven colleges with a recorded namesake, seventeen are religious — Mary Magdalene, John the Baptist, All Souls' Day, the Holy Trinity — and twenty are personal or secular.

78The split tracks time. 79The medieval and Tudor colleges are named for saints and feast days; the modern ones are named for the person who paid — Nuffield, Wolfson, Kellogg, Reuben. 80The colleges' coats of arms encode the same lineage, each crest borrowing the heraldry of its founder or patron.

The newest stone

85The newest college, and the honest holes

86The most recent row is Reuben College, founded in 2019 — the first new college at Oxford since Kellogg in 1990. 87Approved by the university as "Parks College," it was renamed after an £80 million gift and took its first graduate students in 2021, in the former Radcliffe Science Library.
