## Story Spine
**Core claim**: Oxford did not grow its colleges steadily over 800 years — it built them in two great bursts separated by a near-silent Georgian century, and each burst marks a shift in who held power: the medieval Church, the Tudor Crown, and modern industry.
**Tension**: We picture Oxford as ancient and continuous, accreting colleges like rings on a tree. The data shows the opposite — long droughts, sudden surges, and a quarter of its colleges already dead.
**Payoff**: The reader sees the college list not as a static roster but as a seismograph of English power, where even a missing website marks the line between the living and the dissolved.

## Sections

### edt_01: The Argument You Can't Win
**Evidence**: ana_03 | **Context**: det_02

[ana_03] Three Oxford colleges have argued for centuries over which is the oldest, and the data shows why no one will ever win. University College dates itself to 1249, Balliol to 1263, Merton to 1264 — all three packed into fifteen years.

[det_02] Each claim rests on a different test. University College points to a 1249 bequest, the earliest endowment. Balliol claims the longest unbroken occupation of its site since around 1263. Merton was simply the first to be handed a written constitution, in 1264. The dates measure different things, so the argument cannot be settled by counting.

[ana_03] Only Rewley Abbey, founded in 1201 and long since dissolved, predates the trio — and being dead, it does not enter the contest.

### edt_02: Two Waves, One Long Silence
**Evidence**: ana_02, ana_08 | **Context**: det_03, det_04, det_05

[ana_02] Across eight centuries Oxford founded colleges in two great humps, not a steady climb. The 13th century opened with 7; the 16th peaked at 8; then the 18th century produced exactly one. The 19th and 20th centuries each delivered 9 — the busiest centuries in the whole dataset.

[ana_08] Grouped into eras the rhythm is stark: 16 medieval foundations (1201–1499), 8 crammed into the Reformation century, just 4 across the two quiet centuries that followed, and then 20 in the modern expansion from 1870 onward. This is not accumulation. It is bursts and droughts.

[editorial] What separates the waves is the most revealing part of the chart: a trough where almost nothing happens.

### edt_03: The 156-Year Gap
**Evidence**: ana_04 | **Context**: det_05

[ana_04] The longest silence in Oxford's history runs 156 years, from Worcester College in 1714 to Keble College in 1870. For a century and a half the university that invented the college simply stopped making them.

[ana_04] The other large gaps tell the same story of a system that moved in fits: 75 years before the short-lived Greek College in 1699, 51 years before Brasenose reopened the Tudor wave in 1509. The foundations cluster; the empty stretches between them are the real shape of the data.

### edt_04: The Reformation Was a Demolition, Not a Boom
**Evidence**: ana_05, ana_06 | **Context**: det_03

[ana_05] The 16th century looks like a building spree, but much of it was wreckage and renaming. Of all 50 colleges, 11 are defunct or merged — nearly a quarter of the list no longer stands alone.

[det_03] The Reformation dissolved the monastic colleges — Gloucester, Durham, Canterbury — and turned others inside out. Thomas Wolsey's Cardinal College was suppressed when he fell, refounded as King Henry VIII's College, and finally re-established by the king in 1546 as Christ Church.

[ana_06] That collision is written into the founder column. Of 28 colleges with a named founder, 27 are unique individuals — founding a college was a once-in-a-lifetime act. Only three names repeat, and two of them, Wolsey and Henry VIII, converge on the same institution: Christ Church, the survivor that swallowed their failures.

### edt_05: The Modern Wave — Women, Then Graduates
**Evidence**: ana_10, ana_07 | **Context**: det_04, det_05, det_06

[ana_10] The second great hump arrives in two clustered bursts. The 1870s alone produced 5 colleges — Keble and the first women's colleges, Lady Margaret Hall in 1878, Somerville and St Anne's in 1879. The 1960s produced another 5, the surge of new graduate colleges: Linacre, St Catherine's, St Cross, Wolfson, Templeton.

[det_04] The 1870s colleges were radical before they were normal: women attended lectures and sat exams there for over forty years before Oxford would grant them degrees, which finally came in 1920.

[ana_07] As the colleges modernised, so did their names. Of 37 colleges with a recorded namesake, the religious dedications — Mary, the Holy Trinity, a roster of saints — never disappear but slowly cede ground to people and benefactors: Nuffield, Wolfson, Linacre. The honour roll secularises in step with the country.

### edt_06: A Website Is a Death Certificate
**Evidence**: ana_09 | **Context**: det_05, det_07

[ana_09] The dataset hides a perfect tell for which colleges are alive. All 39 current colleges have a website. Of the 11 defunct or merged ones, 10 have none.

[ana_09] The single exception proves the rule: Templeton College's old URL still resolves — but only by redirecting to Green Templeton, the college it merged into in 2008. An 800-year-old institution announces its survival through one modern artifact, a live web address, and a dissolved medieval monastery shares the same blank cell as a 20th-century college that merged away.

[editorial] The list of Oxford's colleges is not a monument. It is a seismograph — of who had money and faith and power, of when the country built and when it stopped, and of which foundations were strong enough to still answer when you knock.

## Editorial Notes
- The three founding years 1249 / 1263 / 1264 must be exact and presented as nominal/disputed, never as settled fact (per ana_caveat_02).
- The 156-year figure (1714→1870) is load-bearing for edt_03 — keep exact.
- 39 current vs 11 defunct, and the website split (39/39 with site; 10/11 defunct without) must be exact.
- Always keep visible that the dataset intentionally includes defunct/merged colleges — this caveat IS the story in edt_04 and edt_06, not a footnote.
- edt_06 is the closing turn; end on the seismograph image, not a summary.
