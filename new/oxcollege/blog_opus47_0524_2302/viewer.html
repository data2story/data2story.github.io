The Hook

5The Argument You Can't Win

6Three Oxford colleges have argued for centuries over which is the oldest, and the data shows why no one will ever win. 7University College dates itself to 1249, Balliol to 1263, Merton to 1264 — all three packed into fifteen years.

8Each claim rests on a different test. 9University College points to a 1249 bequest, the earliest endowment. 10Balliol claims the longest unbroken occupation of its site since around 1263. 11Merton was simply the first to be handed a written constitution, in 1264. 12The dates measure different things, so the argument cannot be settled by counting.

13Only Rewley Abbey, founded in 1201 and long since dissolved, predates the trio — and being dead, it does not enter the contest.

The Shape

16Two Waves, One Long Silence

17Across eight centuries Oxford founded colleges in two great humps, not a steady climb. 18The 13th century opened with 7; the 16th peaked at 8; then the 18th century produced exactly one. 19The 19th and 20th centuries each delivered 9 — the busiest centuries in the whole dataset.

20Grouped into eras the rhythm is stark: 16 medieval foundations (1201–1499), 8 crammed into the Reformation century, just 4 across the two quiet centuries that followed, and then 20 in the modern expansion from 1870 onward. 21This is not accumulation. 22It is bursts and droughts.

23What separates the waves is the most revealing part of the chart: a trough where almost nothing happens.

The Trough

24The 156-Year Gap

25The longest silence in Oxford's history runs 156 years, from Worcester College in 1714 to Keble College in 1870. 26For a century and a half the university that invented the college simply stopped making them.

27The other large gaps tell the same story of a system that moved in fits: 75 years before the short-lived Greek College in 1699, 51 years before Brasenose reopened the Tudor wave in 1509. 28The foundations cluster; the empty stretches between them are the real shape of the data.

The Turn

29The Reformation Was a Demolition, Not a Boom

30The 16th century looks like a building spree, but much of it was wreckage and renaming. 31Of all 50 colleges, 11 are defunct or merged — nearly a quarter of the list no longer stands alone.

5 3211 of 50 33Oxford colleges in this dataset no longer stand alone

34The Reformation dissolved the monastic colleges — Gloucester, Durham, Canterbury — and turned others inside out. 35Thomas Wolsey's Cardinal College was suppressed when he fell, refounded as King Henry VIII's College, and finally re-established by the king in 1546 as Christ Church.

36That collision is written into the founder column. 37Of 28 colleges with a named founder, 27 are unique individuals — founding a college was a once-in-a-lifetime act. 38Only three names repeat, and two of them, Wolsey and Henry VIII, converge on the same institution: Christ Church, the survivor that swallowed their failures.

The Second Wave

39The Modern Wave — Women, Then Graduates

40The second great hump arrives in two clustered bursts. 41The 1870s alone produced 5 colleges — Keble and the first women's colleges, Lady Margaret Hall in 1878, Somerville and St Anne's in 1879. 42The 1960s produced another 5, the surge of new graduate colleges: Linacre, St Catherine's, St Cross, Wolfson, Templeton.

43The 1870s colleges were radical before they were normal: women attended lectures and sat exams there for over forty years before Oxford would grant them degrees, which finally came in 1920.

44As the colleges modernised, so did their names. 45Of 37 colleges with a recorded namesake, the religious dedications — Mary, the Holy Trinity, a roster of saints — never disappear but slowly cede ground to people and benefactors: Nuffield, Wolfson, Linacre. 46The honour roll secularises in step with the country.

The Close

47A Website Is a Death Certificate

48The dataset hides a perfect tell for which colleges are alive. 49All 39 current colleges have a website. 50Of the 11 defunct or merged ones, 10 have none.

51The single exception proves the rule: Templeton College's old URL still resolves — but only by redirecting to Green Templeton, the college it merged into in 2008. 52An 800-year-old institution announces its survival through one modern artifact, a live web address, and a dissolved medieval monastery shares the same blank cell as a 20th-century college that merged away.

53The list of Oxford's colleges is not a monument. 54It is a seismograph — of who had money and faith and power, of when the country built and when it stopped, and of which foundations were strong enough to still answer when you knock.
