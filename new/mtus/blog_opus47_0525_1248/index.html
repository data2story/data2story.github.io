The gap

1,440 minutes, unequally spent

A day is the one thing everyone owns in equal measure: exactly 1,440 minutes, no more for the rich, no fewer for the poor. The question is what we do with them.

The world's largest archive of time-use diaries answers that question, and the answer is lopsided. Women average 236.8 minutes a day of unpaid work — housework, childcare, looking after relatives — to men's 104.9. That is 2.26 times as much. Men do more paid work, 228.3 minutes a day to women's 128.8, but the paid surplus does not erase the unpaid one.

Add both kinds of work together and the ledger tips the other way: women's total working day runs 365.6 minutes, men's 333.2. Women work about half an hour longer, every day — the "second shift," visible in the numbers.

The method

How the diaries work

The data comes from the Multinational Time Use Study, which harmonises 1.4 million diary days from 108 surveys across 25 countries and six decades. Instead of asking people how much they "usually" work or sleep — questions that invite flattering guesses — the diary records what someone was actually doing, minute by minute, across one real day. Every day sums to exactly 1,440 minutes, so every activity competes with every other.

Pooled and weighted to represent populations, the average human day is mostly three things: sleep (506 minutes, 8h26m), screens (143 minutes of television, radio and computer), and paid work (177 minutes). Housework takes another 147. The remaining categories — eating, travel, reading, sport, going out — divide what is left.

These are weighted averages on good-quality diaries, and they mix workers with the retired and the young, which is why population-wide paid work looks modest. The point is the shape of the day, not any one person's.

A tour of 25 flags

Who sleeps, who works

Sleep is not the same everywhere. South Africans sleep most in the data, 566 minutes a day (9h26m); Austrians least, 457 (7h37m) — a spread of nearly an hour and three-quarters. Near the bottom sit the United Kingdom and South Korea, the latter long known as one of the most sleep-starved, overworked societies on earth.

Paid work tells its own geography. Poland, the Czech Republic and Serbia top the population-wide paid-work ranking; the Netherlands and Belgium sit at the bottom, the Dutch figure pulled down by the world's highest rate of part-time work. South Korea's overwork reputation shows up, but below several Central European countries — a reminder that a country's diary average blends employment rates and survey timing, not just how hard its workers grind.

Read these league tables with care: countries were surveyed in different years on harmonised but not identical instruments, so part of every gap is when and how a place was measured.

Inequality at home

The map of inequality at home

The unpaid-work gender gap is a story of place as much as sex. In Peru, women do 290.8 more minutes of unpaid work a day than men — nearly five hours of difference. Serbia, Armenia, the Czech Republic and Poland follow with gaps over 220 minutes.

At the other end sit the United States (a 79.6-minute gap), Finland, Canada and Belgium. The pattern broadly matches what researchers find elsewhere: Nordic and Anglo countries cluster near parity while several Southern, Eastern European and Latin American societies show the widest divides. Nowhere is the gap zero.

The turn

The screen ate the evening

One block of the day grew almost without interruption: the screen. Computer and internet time was effectively zero before the 1990s, then climbed — 5 minutes a day in the 1990s, 14 in the 2000s, 20 in the 2020s. Television never collapsed to make room; it rose too, from about 100 minutes a day in the 1970s to 151 in the 2020s. Total screen time grew from roughly 100 minutes to 171.

Set the modern day beside the 20th-century one and the trade is clear: more time on screens (TV and radio up 46 minutes, computer up 17), less on paid work (down 23) and housework (down 14). Because the diary logs only one activity at a time, the computer figure almost certainly undercounts the scrolling we now layer on top of everything else.

The turn

The gap is closing — because men finally showed up

The unpaid-work gap is narrowing, but not the way the optimistic story goes. It ran about 130 minutes in the 1970s and is down to roughly 89 today. The cause is asymmetric: women's unpaid minutes barely fell, while men's rose from 87 in the 1970s to 134 in the 2020s. The gap closed less because women were freed and more because men finally started doing more at home.

And the total amount of work society does barely moves. Add paid and unpaid work together and the decade totals hover near each other from the 1970s to the 2020s — the "work-time invariance" researchers describe. What changes across sixty years is who does which kind of work, not how much work there is.

Even now the gap is about an hour and a half a day. At the pace of the last half-century, parity is still generations away.

The fairness ledger

The fairness ledger

Time is the most evenly distributed resource on earth and one of the most unevenly spent. The diaries turn an invisible kind of labour — the cooking, the cleaning, the carrying of children — into something countable, and once counted it is hard to unsee.

Women still hand over half an hour more of their day to work of every kind, most of it unpaid and uncounted in any economy's books. The gap is smaller than it was. It is not gone. The next minutes are still up for grabs.
