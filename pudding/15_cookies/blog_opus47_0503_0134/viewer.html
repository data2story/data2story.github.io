5The most-average cookie has bourbon in it

6In May 2018, Elle O'Brien and Amber Thomas at The Pudding scraped 209 chocolate chip cookie recipes off the internet, scaled them all to a 48-cookie yield, and computed the strict arithmetic mean of every ingredient. 7The resulting average cookie contains 68 distinct ingredients. 8Ten of them appear in substantial quantities. 9Forty-six are visible. 10Nineteen are pure trace.

15In there, in microscopic but mathematically real amounts, are bourbon (0.019 tablespoons, from a single recipe), zucchini, applesauce, coriander, white pepper, and a thing labeled simply "nestle." 23 distinct ingredients show up in exactly one recipe out of 209, and the average keeps every one of them.

21O'Brien actually baked the result. Her verdict: 22"Chewy and very chocolatey, no one would suspect these cookies were made with everything in your pantry." 23The average cookie tastes fine. 24It also has trace marshmallows in it.

25But the average cookie is also boringly normal

26If you only look at the top of the list, the average cookie is unsurprisingly Toll House. 27Per 48 cookies: 3.5 cups all-purpose flour, 2.9 eggs, 1.8 cups semisweet chips, 1.4 teaspoons baking soda, 1.2 cups light brown sugar, 1.1 teaspoons salt, 1.1 cups butter, and 1 cup white sugar. 28Vanilla, three teaspoons of it. 29That's the cookie Ruth Wakefield invented at her Toll House Inn in 1938, after eight decades of distributed amateur engineering by anonymous home bakers.

31Nine ingredients appear in three quarters or more of the 209 recipes — egg (97.6%), vanilla (93.8%), all-purpose flour (92.3%), baking soda (89.5%), white sugar (83.3%), light brown sugar (81.3%), salt (79.9%), butter (76.1%), and semisweet chocolate chip (74.6%). 32After this group, there's a cliff: the next ingredient, baking powder, appears in only 23.9% of recipes. There is no middle. 33An ingredient is either canonical or a one-baker quirk.

45This is the structure that makes "the average" misleading. 46The arithmetic mean treats each of those rare ingredients as a tiny, real number — not as zero, not as missing, not as an outlier — and rolls them all into the recipe. 47White pepper, in microscopic doses, becomes part of the cookie.

48Where the bakers actually disagree

49The standardized form of the cookie hides a wider disagreement underneath. 50Among the 192 recipes that include all-purpose flour, the median is 3.3 cups (per 48 cookies) but the spread runs from 1.5 cups at the 5th percentile to 8 cups at the 95th. 51The widest recipe lists 16 cups. 52The narrowest, 0.32. 53Flour is the ingredient where bakers seem least sure how much is enough.

73The oven hasn't moved in 80 years

74Dump every recipe's directions into one corpus and grep for oven temperatures. 75350 degrees Fahrenheit gets 74 mentions. 76375 gets 46. 77Everything else — 325, 300, 385 — combined accounts for fewer than 11. 78About 60% of all temperature mentions land on 350F, the temperature Wakefield's original Toll House recipe specified in 1938.

91What averaging is actually for

92Beyond the cookie itself, this is a small argument about what we ask "the average" to do. 93Recommendation algorithms, predictive text, neural networks — they all promise to summarize a population and return a representative output. 94O'Brien's three experiments tested this on cookies. 95The mathematical mean produced an edible 68-ingredient Frankenstein. 96The 4-gram predictive text model fell into an infinite loop the moment it encountered cannelini beans. 97The character-level neural network output "1.904 cups seconds" and listed white sugar five separate times.

98Average is not typical. 99Average is not preferred. 100Average is what you get when you let the union of every minority taste have a vote. 101Sometimes — as with the cookies — the result is fine. 102Sometimes — as with the neural net — it's nonsense. 103The cookie experiment is a small, tactile demonstration of a thing that is harder to feel when the topic is news feeds or movie recommendations: the algorithm is summing all of us, including the one person who put zucchini in their cookies, and pretending that's what the population wanted.
