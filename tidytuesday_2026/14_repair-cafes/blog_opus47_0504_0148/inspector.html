Repair Cafes · 2015 — 2026

2What 178,749 Broken Things Tell Us

3A volunteer-collected ledger of every item brought to a Repair Cafe shows that whether a thing can be saved is mostly a property of its design — and a tiny but rising chorus of fixers is now asking ChatGPT for help.

By way of Repair Monitor · TidyTuesday 2026 · Week 14

01 · The split6Sewing wins, circuits lose

7Volunteers at Repair Cafes around the world have logged 178,749 repair attempts since 2015. 8Sixty-three percent of those items got fully fixed. 9Another 13% got partly fixed. 10About a quarter — 43,112 specific objects — were declared dead on arrival.

11Whether your thing makes it through depends almost entirely on what kind of thing it is. 12Knives and scissors are saved 97.5% of the time. 13Trousers, 96.0%. 14Garden shears, 95.4%. 15Printers, 36.8%. 16Electric kettles, 39.9%. 17The dataset draws a hard line: things you can sharpen, sew, or unscrew end up fixed; things with sealed circuit boards end up in landfill.

18This is what planned obsolescence looks like in microcosm. 19Each of those 178,749 rows is one person's small attempt to reject the throw-away default — and one volunteer's note about whether the object's design let them succeed.

02 · Origin32From Amsterdam-West to a global hockey stick

33On 18 October 2009, in a converted theater in Amsterdam-West, a Dutch journalist named Martine Postma held the first Repair Cafe. 34She had become preoccupied, after the birth of her second child, with how much serviceable stuff Europeans throw out. 35The fix she invented was social: invite neighbors with broken things, pair them with volunteer fixers, serve coffee.

36Sixteen years later there are nearly 3,200 Repair Cafes in more than forty countries. 37Many log every item they touch into Repair Monitor, the foundation's voluntary dashboard. 38The TidyTuesday 2026 dataset is the raw row-level export of that dashboard — 447 branches across 25 countries, including the free-text fields where volunteers write what was wrong, what they did, and what stopped them.

39Activity has compounded into a hockey stick. 40Twenty-eight repairs were logged in 2015. 41By 2019 that grew to 15,491. 42COVID closures cut volume in half during 2020 and 2021, then growth resumed: 29,406 repairs in 2023, 39,981 in 2024, 45,165 in 2025. 43Volunteer reach roughly doubles every two years.

03 · The door48What everyone brings to the cafe

49Two items dominate the door: coffee makers (10,770 attempts) and vacuum cleaners (10,284). 50After that come trousers (6,769), bicycles (4,887), and sewing machines (4,614). 51The list is sticky — the foundation reported the same two leaders in their 2018 analysis.

52The Netherlands accounts for 50.8% of all logged repairs in this dataset. 53Half of those coffee makers are Dutch coffee makers, and the most common Dutch coffee maker is a Senseo. 54Read the rankings with that in mind: this is what breaks in the rich world, weighted toward the country that invented the cafe.

04 · The wall60It's the parts, mostly

61Take vacuum cleaners. 62Volunteers logged 10,284 attempts and salvaged 61% of them. 63Of the failures, the largest single tag is just "No way to fix the product" (7.5%). 64Right behind it: "Spare parts not available at repair session" (7.3%), "Spare parts too expensive" (6.0%), "Too worn out" (5.9%), and — telling — "No way to open the product" (3.8%). 65Modern vacuums are built with plastic clips and proprietary screws that resist amateur disassembly.

66Across all 65,999 failed and half-fixed records, the same pattern holds. 67Spare-parts problems collectively (not at session, not on market, too expensive) account for 9,042 tags — the single largest barrier when grouped together. 68The 2018 foundation analysis warned about this: in 46% of unsuccessful repairs, the blocker was a non-replaceable broken part. 69Six years later, parts are still the wall.

70The pattern divides cleanly along electrical lines. 71In electric tools and electric household appliances, 7–8% of all attempts (not just failed ones) get blocked by missing or expensive parts. 72In textile, that number is 0.5%. The EU's new Right-to-Repair Directive 2024/1799 — entering force in member states by July 2026 — was written for exactly this asymmetry: it requires manufacturers of covered products to make spare parts and repair information available "within a reasonable time and price."

05 · Brand80When brand is destiny

81Holding the product type fixed and looking just at brands, the gap is stark. 82Among vacuum cleaners with at least 100 logged attempts, Henry succeeds 69.2% of the time. 83Miele, 66.0%. 84Philips, 64.8%. Dyson — the brand most associated with premium price and proprietary design — sits last, at 47.4%, across 1,202 attempts. 86Among coffee makers, Philips (the Senseo) leads at 59.5%; Braun comes last at 30.9%.

87Volunteer brand strings are noisy and Dyson units may also skew newer. But the spread — twenty-two percentage points between top and bottom — is too large to wave away as sampling chance. 89There is a real, measurable cost to design choices that lock fixers out.

06 · AgeWhy old things win

97Plot success rate against the item's age and you get a U-shape. Brand-new items (0–1 years old) get fixed 67% of the time. Then success drops, bottoming out around 6–15 years (around 56%). 100Then it climbs again. Items 21–30 years old are repaired 61% of the time. Items 31–50 years old, 62%. 103Items over fifty, 63%.

104The vintage rebound makes physical sense once you read what those old items are. 105Of the 14,112 items in the dataset aged twenty years or more, the top categories are sewing machines (1,212), clocks (553), vacuum cleaners (483), radios (471), turntables (356), and bicycles (331). 106These are pre-throwaway-era objects, made when steel cost less than electronics, with replaceable parts and accessible internals. 107They were built to last and they are still standing.

07 · AI114The chatbot enters the cafe

115Volunteers can record where they got their repair information. 116YouTube has been the dominant external source since the dashboard began — about half a percent of all logged repairs cite it as a source, fairly stable across years. 117Generative AI is brand new. 118Zero mentions before September 2023. 119Two mentions in 2023. 120Eight in 2024. 121Fourteen in 2025. 122Two in the first eight months of 2026.

123The verbatim values are revealing. 124Volunteers wrote it as "chat gpt", "Chat GPT", "CHAT GPT", "chatgpt", "Chat gpt" — six different capitalizations of the same product. 125Others logged "WWW.OPENai.COM", "AI Perplexity", "perplexity et manuel" (using AI alongside a paper manual), and one even pasted a complete OpenAI conversation share URL. 126YouTube is named consistently across thousands of rows. 127AI is so new that volunteers have no agreed-upon name for it.

The total volume — 26 AI mentions versus 843 YouTube mentions — is tiny. 129But this is the first time a volunteer-collected, time-stamped, global dataset has captured the entry of generative AI into the practical toolkit of amateur fixers. 130The graph is not large; it is just the beginning of one.

08 · TrousersHemming is repair

142One of the cleanest stories in the data hides inside the third-most-common item. 143Trousers and pants got 6,769 attempts, and 96% of them ended in success. 144Read the defect text and you find out why: "te lang" (615 times), "korter maken" (195), "pijpen te lang" (99), "too long" (87). 145The dominant trouser "repair" is hemming. People are bringing in clothing to be altered — to fit a body — not because anything is broken.

147This is its own quiet finding. 148The textile category has a 92.3% success rate, and a sizable share of that is alteration disguised as repair. 149The line between "fix" and "tailor" turns out to be blurry; the line between "fixable" and "unfixable" is much sharper, and it is drawn by the manufacturer.

09 · Voices157Field notes from the failure column

158The free-text "why we couldn't fix it" field, read in bulk, is unexpectedly moving. A coffee maker: 159"product is van inferieure kwaliteit, slecht te repareren" — the product is of inferior quality, badly repairable. 161A printer: "Vital Component Failure. 162Not available to purchase." A laptop: 163"Het apparaat is te oud om nog te repareren" — the device is too old to be repaired. A radio: 165"Schema nodig, niet te vinden" — circuit diagram needed, cannot be found. 167An amplifier the volunteer dragged into the cafe: 168"versterker heeft computer die alles checkt, maar wil niet starten, ook vol sigarenrook" — amp has a computer that checks everything but won't start, also full of cigar smoke.

170These are not statistics; they are tiny ethnographic field notes. 171Each row is a small story about a specific object meeting a specific obstacle. 172The Repair Monitor dataset is rare in capturing this voice at all — manufacturer warranty data never records what the user thinks happened.

10 · CarbonCounting the carbon

191The Repair Cafe Foundation, drawing on a master's-thesis estimate by British researcher Steve Privett, says one successful Repair Cafe fix prevents about 24 kilograms of CO₂ emissions — mostly from the manufacture of the replacement that didn't have to be made. 192Apply that figure to the 112,776 successful repairs in this dataset and you get 2,707 tonnes of CO₂ prevented. 193The cumulative line tracks the volume curve: a tonne by 2015, 213 tonnes by 2018, 1,287 by 2023, 2,594 by 2025.

194That is one community-collected sample of one global movement. The foundation estimates the full network — 3,200 cafes — could save more than 8.5 million kilos of CO₂ a year if all branches were active. 196Three months after the most recent rows in this dataset, EU member states must transpose Right-to-Repair Directive 2024/1799 into law. 197The same volunteers who wrote 198"Schema nodig, niet te vinden" — circuit diagram needed, cannot be found — finally have a regulator behind them.

11 · Close207Fixable is a design choice

208Volunteers also rate each item on a 1-to-10 repairability scale — easy to fix, hard to fix. 209The score is monotonic: items rated 1 are fixed 13.7% of the time, items rated 10 are fixed 85.8%. 210The repairability score is, in effect, a volunteer-given anti-obsolescence rating. It works.

211If you remember one thing from this dataset, make it this: fixable is not a property of the object. 212It is a property of the design. 213Trousers in the 1880s and trousers in the 2020s have the same fixability — needle and thread will hem either of them. 214A coffee maker in the 1980s and a coffee maker in the 2020s do not. 215The Repair Cafe data, row by row, is the largest community-collected proof of where that gap is, what it costs us, and which manufacturers are quietly responsible.
